## Part 1: Perez's framework, from primary sources

### Key definitions (verbatim)

- **Technological revolution** (Perez 2002, quoted by Perez in Project Syndicate, 11 Mar 2024): "A technological revolution can be defined as a powerful and highly visible cluster of new and dynamic technologies, products and industries, capable of bringing about an upheaval in the whole fabric of the economy and of propelling a long-term upsurge of development. It is a strongly interrelated constellation of technical innovations, generally including an important all-pervasive low-cost input, often a source of energy, sometimes a crucial material, plus significant new products and processes and a new infrastructure." [HIGH, [author PDF](https://carlotaperez.org/wp-content/downloads/media/articles-and-blogs/On-AI-by-CP-in-Project-Syndicate.pdf)]
- **Great surge of development** (Perez 2002, p.15, quoted by Perez on 24 Feb 2017): "the process by which a technological revolution and its paradigm propagate across the economy, leading to structural changes in production, distribution, communication and consumption as well as to profound and qualitative changes in society." [HIGH, [carlotaperez.org](https://carlotaperez.org/second-machine-age-or-fifth-technological-revolution-part-2/)]
- **Revolutionary technology vs technological revolution** (2024): "a revolutionary technology is not the same thing as a technological revolution." [HIGH]
- **Three tests for a revolution** (Lebdioui interview, *Development and Change*, published online Oct 2025, DOI 10.1111/dech.70007):
  - "the first is its capacity to increase productivity and reduce costs across the economy."
  - "The second is the strong synergy among the knowledge bases and the skills required to use them and to innovate with them."
  - "The third is the provision of a new infrastructure."
  
  [HIGH, [PDF](https://carlotaperez.org/wp-content/downloads/media/interviews-and-podcasts/as-interviewee/Development-and-Change-2025-Lebdioui.pdf)]

### 1c. Life-cycle phases, bubbles, crashes and golden ages

**Sequence.** Perez (2009), verbatim [HIGH]: "irruption of the revolution, two or three decades of a turbulent Installation period ending in a major bubble collapse, then a recomposition of the socio-institutional framework that regulates finance and sets the conditions for the final Deployment period, a time of more organic growth that lasts until maturity and exhaustion are reached."

**Phase names.** The 2002 book divides each surge into irruption and frenzy (installation), then the turning point, then synergy and maturity (deployment). The Wikipedia summary of these phases is muddled: it describes "synergy" as the moment the bubble bursts. Do not reuse it. [MEDIUM]

**Maturity dates.** The texts opened give no separate maturity dates. Perez & Murray Leach (2022) give only each surge's full span (1771–1828, 1829–1874, 1875–1907, 1908–1970), so the late years of each span are the maturity, as she defines it.

**The double bubble.** Perez (2009, abstract) [HIGH]: the dot-com and 2000s booms "are two distinct components of a single structural phenomenon. They are essentially the equivalent of 1929 developed in two stages, one centred on technological innovation, the other on financial innovation."

**Precursor bubbles.** Perez (2009) [HIGH]: "The early boom episodes in the Installation of ICT peaked and collapsed in 1983 and 1987." Such "'precursor bubbles'" have a recovery that "is relatively swift," which "actually serves to strengthen confidence for when the real bubble builds up." This matters for interpretation A in Part 4.

**Scale of past bubbles** (Perez 2009, HIGH):
- "In 1847, at the peak of the major bubble of the second surge, it is estimated that U.K. investment in railways reached 7% of national income and nearly 55% of gross national fixed capital formation" (citing Mitchell 1964 and Deane 1968).
- "from the 1870s to the 1890s, between 30 and 50% of all British investment went overseas."
- "venture capital alone ... amassed funds that grew to as much as 1% of US GDP at the peak of the boom. Of these, between 85 and 90% went to projects in information and telecommunications technologies."
- "At the peak of the boom the technology stocks represented as much as 35% of total market capitalisation, and Internet alone almost 10%."

### 1d. The key-factor criteria

Perez (1985), *World Development* 13(3): 441–63 [HIGH, [PDF](https://carlotaperez.org/wp-content/downloads/publications/development-s-g/WD_microelec_longwaves.pdf)]. The "key factor" is an input "capable of playing a steering role because it fulfills the following conditions":

1. "clearly perceived low — and descending — relative cost,"
2. "apparently unlimited supply (for all practical purposes)." The PDF's OCR reads "for ah practical purposes".
3. "obvious potential for all-pervasive influence in the productive sphere, and"
4. "a generally recognized capacity, based on a set of interwoven technical and organizational innovations, to reduce the costs and change the quality of capital equipment, labor and products."

Two further passages:
- 1985, main text: "The truly new aspect in each case is the drastic reduction in relative cost." In footnote 13, for ICT, electronics "only became an all pervasive key factor when its original control functions fused in synergetic fashion with data processing."
- Perez (2004), *Technological revolutions, paradigm shifts and socio-institutional change*: the key input "is very cheap, offers to remain cheap and, in conjunction with a constellation of generic innovations, radically transforms -in its favor- the relative cost structure." [HIGH, [PDF](https://carlotaperez.org/wp-content/downloads/publications/organizational-change/TRs_TEP_shifts_and_SIF_ch.pdf)]

### 1e. Financial vs production capital, decoupling, recoupling, the state, golden-age requirements

- **Decoupling.** Perez (2009) [HIGH]: "asset inflation takes off when the actors in the financial markets clearly switch from seeking dividends to pursuing capital gains, which results in the paper economy decoupling from the real one." The collapse "signals the end of a period when financial capital is in control of investment to a period in which control passes over to production capital."
- **Two defining traits of a major technology bubble.** Perez (2009) [HIGH]: "(a) their concentration on the new technologies –especially the new infrastructural networks– and (b) their decoupling from the real economy."
- **Necessary over-investment.** Perez (2016) [HIGH]: the frenzy "facilitates a necessary over-investment in the new infrastructures, in order that coverage (whether of canals, railways or the Internet) is broad enough for widespread usage."
- **The state after the crash.** Perez (2016) [HIGH]: "before this can occur, finance has typically been regulated and reoriented so that it serves the production economy once again ... after the major collapses, the state has historically stepped in to play an active role in favour of investment and growth."
- **Three areas of state action behind every golden age.** Lebdioui (2025) [HIGH]:
  1. "providing directionality while equipping the state for the new tasks";
  2. "handling the powerful, in the sense of regulating the monopolistic giants as well as getting finance to recouple with production";
  3. "the fostering of the common good."
- **What golden ages are.** Lebdioui (2025) [HIGH]: "they give direction to the available technological potential by setting up a win-win game between business and society."
- **Synergy as the hallmark.** Perez (2016) [HIGH]: "these synergies between the new technologies, industries and infrastructures ... are the hallmark of a technological revolution."

**Her position, stated plainly.**
- AI is a revolutionary technology inside the fifth (ICT) surge, possibly its "third leap". It is not a sixth revolution.
- The ICT surge is stuck in an unusually long installation / turning point.
- A crash is historically to be expected before any golden age.
- The golden age requires state directionality ("smart, green, fair and global") and the recoupling of finance with production.
- AI could seed an eventual sixth revolution combined with biotech and new materials.

**Misreadings to avoid.** Some search summaries say Perez places AI in a "frenzied deployment stage". That wording is not in the FT/Irish Times text. "As for AI, we remain in that manic installation phase" is Thornhill's sentence, not Perez's quote. The Irish Times piece also misstates Perez's dates, giving "steam ... of the 1830s" where she uses 1829. That is minor.

### 4b. Key-factor test against Perez's four 1985 criteria

| Criterion | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1. "clearly perceived low — and descending — relative cost" | At fixed capability the price fell 9x–900x a year (Epoch, Mar 2025). GPT-3.5-level tokens per $ rose about 286x from Nov 2022 to Oct 2024. GPT-4-level tokens per $ rose about 75x from Nov 2023 to Oct 2026. **Result.** Cheap tokens meet two of Perez's four criteria today and are trending on a third (cost). Note also that tokens are made from chips, whose cost decline is Perez's fifth-surge key factor itself. Epoch's 49%-a-year chip price-performance continues the microelectronics curve. That supports interpretation B.

### 4c. Timing arithmetic

- **History.** From big bang to major crash took 15–22 years in surges 1–4 and 29 years in surge 5 (to 2000).
- **If ChatGPT (2022) is the big bang**, 2026 is year 4. A historical-scale frenzy would be very early. In Perez's own scheme, a 2026–27 correction would more likely be a precursor bubble, like ICT's 1983 and 1987 episodes, than the installation-ending crash.
- **If AlexNet (2012) is the big bang**, 2026 is year 14. That is in range for a late frenzy.
- **If the Transformer (2017) is the big bang**, 2026 is year 9.
- **Within surge 5 (interpretation B)**, 2026 is year 55 since 1971. That is at the outer edge of Perez's 50–60-year surge length, which is why she says the ICT installation and turning point have "broken" the record.

### 4d. The three interpretations

**(A) AI is a sixth surge, in installation and now frenzy.**
- **Strongest argument.** The cluster is complete:
  - a new cheap input: capability-adjusted inference cost falling an order of magnitude or more each year;
  - a new infrastructure: GW-scale data centres plus power, with big-five capex of $448B in 2025, about 1.5% of US GDP and above the 2000 telecom peak;
  - Perez's two bubble signatures, concentration and decoupling. AI took ~72% of global VC and 82.7% of US VC deal value in Jan–Sep 2026. Private labs are valued near $1T against revenue of tens of billions. There is supplier-financed demand (Nvidia–OpenAI), off-balance-sheet SPVs (Hyperion) and $194B of hyperscaler bonds by July 2026.
  
  Mechanising "mental labour" is a different territory from the four revolutions that mechanised manual labour; Perez herself says so (2022). Many 2026 commentators read it this way (Wee, Schnabl, Görgen).
- **Weaknesses.** The key input is still microelectronics. The infrastructure rides on the internet and the cloud. The leaders are the fifth surge's incumbents, not new entrants funded by financial capital. A 2022 big bang makes the frenzy historically premature. And Perez explicitly rejects this reading.

**(B) AI is a late chapter of the fifth (ICT) surge. This is Perez's position.**
- **Strongest argument.**
  - AI "depends on the internet, which in turn depends on powerful microprocessors and computers" (2024).
  - She lists AI among the fifth surge's industries (2022) and calls it "the latest addition" (2025).
  - The key factor is still the ever-cheaper chip.
  - The principles AI intensifies (information intensity, data as raw material, platforms) are those of ICT.
  - Most of the capex has come from fifth-surge giants' operating cash flow, which is production capital.
  - Her analogies, plastics within mass production and electricity within the steel age, are revolutionary technologies inside a surge.
  - Her reading of the present is an over-long turning point awaiting institutional recomposition. The data fit: no finance-recoupling regulation, rising inequality, and populism, which she calls "typical of a mid-way turning point".
  - On this view the AI boom is a third bubble in the ICT turning point, and "I have not seen a golden age happening without a crash" implies its bust could finally force the recomposition.
- **Weaknesses.** None of the four previous surges had a new, investment-scale technology boom inside its turning point. Since 2025 financing has shifted toward debt and SPVs, a financial-capital pattern rather than a production-capital one. Under B the 2025–26 build-out is anomalous.

**(C) Hybrid.** AI is a revolutionary technology within ICT whose financial dynamics replay installation, and possibly the forerunner of a sixth surge.
- **Strongest argument.** Perez's own text supports both halves: AI is "an integral part of the current digital revolution, and it could also lay the groundwork for breakthroughs in biotech, nanotech, and other fields that may be at the center of an eventual sixth revolution" (2024). Her precedent fits: Bessemer steel emerged "in the 1860s, midway through the second revolution", was "impressive and transformative, but not yet a revolution", and became the key factor of the third once the open-hearth process made it cheap. The data show both A-type signals (cost collapse, frenzy finance) and B-type signals (chip dependence, incumbent leadership, modest measured productivity, failure of the unlimited-supply test).
- **Weaknesses.** C is hard to falsify. It needs an explicit marker: a non-ICT cluster (AI with biotech and materials) acquiring its own cheap input and infrastructure.

**Which one Perez holds:** B, with a C-type caveat about a possible sixth revolution. She has said so in print three times since March 2024: Project Syndicate (11 Mar 2024), the FT interview as reported (23 Aug 2025), and *Development and Change* (Oct 2025). Her 20 Jun 2025 slides say the same. She has not, in any source found, called AI a sixth revolution.

**Report AI's working conclusion for the published piece (DERIVED).**
- Present B as Perez's view, with quotes, and present A as the popular reading, labelled as such.
- Use C as the analytical frame. It is the one most consistent with both the data and her Bessemer-steel precedent.
- Make the key-factor test the centrepiece: cheap intelligence passes on cost and pervasiveness, fails on supply, and is unproven on productivity.

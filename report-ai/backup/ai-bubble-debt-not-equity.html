Home / Reports / Dark Side of AI / The AI Bubble Runs on Debt

The dot-com boom was paid for mostly with equity — IPO cash and soaring share prices that vaporized shareholders, not lenders, when the marquee internet names collapsed. The AI infrastructure build-out is different: it is being paid for with debt — investment-grade bonds, private credit, and off-balance-sheet data-center vehicles. That structural shift is why some analysts argue an AI unwind could be more contagious than 2000. The clearest stress signal so far: Wisconsin regulators have ordered Oracle to post roughly $7 billion in collateral before it can power a giant AI data center in the state — a demand triggered directly by Oracle's July 2026 credit downgrade. That figure was first reported by the Financial Times and corroborated across multiple outlets; it is verified, not merely podcast chatter.

The dot-com boom was equity. This one is debt.

The canonical 1990s internet bubble was an equity story. Companies like Pets.com raised money by selling shares to a public desperate for growth; when the market turned, the losses landed on shareholders, who have no claim on a company's cash flows and no covenant to enforce. Painful, but self-contained — equity is designed to absorb loss. Today's AI infrastructure boom is being financed the opposite way. Hyperscalers and neoclouds are funding hundreds of billions in GPUs, land, and power with borrowed money: corporate bonds, syndicated loans, private credit, and special-purpose vehicles (SPVs) that ring-fence a single data-center campus.

The distinction matters because debt is contractual. It carries fixed coupons, maturity dates, and covenants that can force asset sales at the worst possible moment. Career analysts at the U.S. Treasury and the Bank for International Settlements have both flagged the shift from internally funded, equity-heavy spending toward debt-financed AI capex as a source of systemic risk (Oliver Wyman, Jan 2026). One honest caveat up front: the 1990s telecom build-out — WorldCom, Global Crossing — was itself heavily debt-financed and did end in a credit crisis. So "dot-com was all equity" is a simplification. The sharper claim is that the famous internet-equity wipeout hit shareholders, while today's build-out puts lenders and credit markets closer to the center of the risk.

How AI data centers are actually financed

Data-center debt issuance nearly doubled to roughly $182 billion in 2025, up from about $92 billion a year earlier, with Meta and Google among the most active issuers (CNBC / Introl, Dec 2025). That is only the visible, on-market slice. Morgan Stanley estimates the global build-out will require at least $1.5 trillion of debt financing through 2028 — of which roughly $800 billion is expected to come from private credit, much of it structured through asset-backed SPVs tied to specific campuses (Morgan Stanley, "Bridging a $1.5tr Data Center Financing Gap"). Private credit and SPVs are the important twist: they move obligations off the parent's balance sheet, so headline leverage understates true exposure. Our companion report, AI's Hidden Debt, has the full breakdown of those off-balance-sheet obligations.

Oracle: the most-leveraged bet in the boom

No large-cap embodies the debt model more than Oracle. On July 9, 2026, S&P Global Ratings cut Oracle to BBB- from BBB — the lowest investment-grade rung, one notch above junk — citing its AI build-out and extreme customer concentration. S&P put Oracle's total debt near $167 billion and forecast a free-operating-cash-flow deficit of roughly $42 billion in fiscal 2027, with capex guided to $90–$95 billion (S&P; ppc.land, Jul 2026). The engine of that spend is Oracle's roughly $300 billion compute commitment to OpenAI under the Stargate arrangement; S&P notes OpenAI accounts for about half of Oracle's ~$638 billion in remaining performance obligations — concentration in a single, cash-burning counterparty (MLQ, Jul 2026).

Markets have repriced the risk in real time. Oracle's five-year credit-default-swap spread has blown out from under 50 basis points before the OpenAI deal to multi-year highs — Seeking Alpha reported it hitting a cycle high near 198 bps — and the stock is down sharply on the year (Seeking Alpha; The Motley Fool, 2026). Margins are the open question: internal documents reported by Data Center Dynamics pegged Oracle's average AI-cloud profit margin at about 16%, with some deals as thin as 14%, even as management guides OCI gross margins to the 30–40% range as it scales (DCD; Morningstar). Thin margins plus heavy fixed debt service is a fragile combination if AI demand or pricing softens.

The Wisconsin collateral standoff

The downgrade had an immediate, concrete cost. In Port Washington, Wisconsin, Oracle is building an AI campus expected to draw nearly a gigawatt of power — part of the OpenAI/Stargate commitment. Under utility We Energies' "large load" tariff, designed to shield residential ratepayers, a customer rated below A- must post collateral covering the net book value of the power plants and dedicated transmission built to serve it. Oracle's cut to BBB- tripped that threshold, and the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin let a collateral requirement of roughly $7 billion stand — with the annual cost of maintaining the letters of credit alone estimated above $100 million. Oracle offered to post about $700 million (roughly 10%), was refused, and has taken the matter to court (Ozaukee County Circuit Court).

Why debt makes a bust worse than 2000

Debt turns a slowdown into a chain reaction in ways equity does not. First, refinancing risk: bonds and private-credit facilities mature and must be rolled over; if AI revenue disappoints while rates stay high, borrowers refinance into a hostile market or default. Second, rate sensitivity: long-lived, low-margin assets financed with floating or short-dated debt are acutely exposed to the cost of money. Third, forced selling and contagion: covenants, collateral calls (exactly what Wisconsin just triggered), and SPV cross-defaults can compel fire-sales that spread losses to banks, insurers, and private-credit funds holding the paper. When the loss sits in credit markets rather than in retail equity, it travels — which is precisely why regulators are watching. In 2000, a falling stock mostly hurt the people who owned it; a data-center SPV that can't pay hurts everyone who lent to it.

The counter-case — and how worried to be

The bear case is not the whole story. Much of this debt is genuinely investment-grade and backed by contracted revenue — unlike the dot-com "future bandwidth" that was pure projection, hyperscaler backlogs are signed remaining performance obligations. Borrowing to build is rational economics when expected returns exceed the cost of capital, and Oracle's OpenAI backlog, whatever its concentration risk, is real contracted demand, not vaporware. Oracle's stock has also, at times, shrugged off the downgrade, and a February 2026 financing plan briefly tightened its CDS as investor confidence returned (CNBC, Feb 2026). The commentary poles should be read as what they are: critics like Ed Zitron frame the build-out as an unsustainable, margin-destroying bubble, while Scott Galloway and Ed Elson on Prof G Markets have highlighted Oracle's leverage as a flashing warning — both are opinion, not settled fact.

How worried to be: the honest answer is "watchful, not panicked." The structural concern — that debt, private credit, and SPVs make this cycle more contagious than the equity-driven dot-com bust — is real and flagged by serious institutions. But the base case is not imminent collapse; it is elevated fragility that depends on AI revenue arriving fast enough to service the debt. Oracle's Wisconsin standoff is the canary: a credit downgrade converting directly into a multibillion-dollar cash demand. Whether it stays an isolated dispute or becomes a template is the question worth watching.

Frequently asked questions

Is the AI boom financed with debt or equity?

Predominantly debt. Data-center debt issuance nearly doubled to about $182 billion in 2025, and Morgan Stanley projects the build-out will need at least $1.5 trillion of debt through 2028, roughly $800 billion of it from private credit and SPVs. That contrasts with the dot-com era, whose marquee internet losses fell on equity shareholders.

Is Oracle overextended on AI?

By credit-market measures, it is stretched. S&P cut Oracle to BBB- (one notch above junk) on July 9, 2026, citing about $167 billion in debt, a projected ~$42 billion cash-flow deficit in FY2027, and heavy concentration in OpenAI, which accounts for roughly half of its ~$638 billion backlog. Bulls counter that the backlog is contracted revenue and the debt largely investment-grade.

Did Wisconsin really demand $7 billion in collateral from Oracle?

Yes — as reported by the Financial Times in July 2026 and corroborated by multiple outlets. After Oracle's downgrade below the A- threshold in utility We Energies' large-load tariff, regulators required roughly $7 billion in collateral for its Port Washington AI campus. Oracle offered about $700 million and is challenging the ruling in court, so figures may change.

1. Capability

The benchmarks that measured AI progress in 2024 were saturated by mid-2026, and the frontier is now measured on tests built to resist it.

Between December 2024 and September 2026 the three benchmarks most often used to show AI progress ran out of headroom. On SWE-bench Verified, a set of 500 human-validated software-engineering tasks drawn from real GitHub issues, the top reported score went from 71.7% for OpenAI's o3 preview in December 2024 to 80.9% for Claude Opus 4.5 on 24 November 2025, and to 97.0% for Claude Opus 5 on Vals.ai's independent leaderboard on 1 September 2026. Vals then stopped running the test on new releases. On GPQA Diamond, graduate-level science questions, the top score went from 87.7% (o3 preview, December 2024) to 93.8% (Gemini 3 Deep Think, December 2025) and 94.3% (Gemini 3.1 Pro, February 2026). Expert review suggests only about 90–95% of GPQA Diamond's questions are valid, so scores in the mid-90s are at the ceiling.

MMLU, the general-knowledge test that served as the reference measure from 2020, had already stopped separating models in 2024, when OpenAI's o1 reported 92.3% against a practical ceiling of about 95%. By the first quarter of 2026 every frontier system scored above 92%, and model cards had moved to its harder successor, MMLU-Pro, on which DeepSeek V4-Pro reported 87.5 in April 2026. Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index describes the same pattern in its own terms: SWE-bench Verified scores rose from about 60% to nearly 100% of the human baseline in one year, and Terminal-Bench, an agentic command-line test, rose from 20% in 2025 to 77.3% in 2026. Each new benchmark is now reaching high scores faster than its designers expected.

The tests designed to resist saturation are also moving, though less cleanly. Humanity's Last Exam, a set of expert-written questions released in January 2025, opened with the best models near 9%. Without tools, Gemini 3 Deep Think reported 41.0% in December 2025 (another account gives 45.1%) and 48.4% in February 2026. Scale's leaderboard showed OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra at 54.8% on 17 October 2026, while a mirror of a September snapshot shows 60.6%. On ARC-AGI-2, a visual-reasoning test launched in March 2025, the verified best rose from 54% (Poetiq, December 2025) to 84.6% (Gemini 3 Deep Think, February 2026). Its successor, ARC-AGI-3, is the one widely reported test on which frontier models still score below 1%.

Two cautions apply to every number in this chapter. First, most scores are self-reported by vendors on their own scaffolding, and the 2026 SWE-bench figure comes from a different harness than the 2024–25 vendor figures, so the line in Exhibit 1 joins readings that are close but not identical in method. Second, high benchmark scores do not mean reliable performance on real work. Stanford HAI's 2026 report stresses a jagged frontier: models that clear PhD-level science questions still fail at tasks that are simple for people, and robots built on the same models succeed at only 12% of real household tasks. Saturation tells us the tests are exhausted, not that the problems they stood for are solved.

Why benchmark scores are not comparable across sources

A benchmark score depends on more than the model. The harness (the code that feeds tasks to the model and checks answers), whether tools such as web search are allowed, how many attempts are permitted, and whether the result was verified by a third party all change the number. On SWE-bench Verified in mid-2026, Vals.ai, BenchLM and vendor model cards reported scores for overlapping models that differed by up to nine points. We therefore plot only like-for-like readings, state the basis of each, and mark a value CONTESTED when credible sources disagree.

2. Releases

Frontier releases accelerated to one significant model every two to three weeks in 2026, and for the first time a release could be withdrawn by government order.

The pace of frontier releases quickened across the three years. In 2024 the notable releases arrived about every six weeks: Claude 3 in March, GPT-4o in May, Claude 3.5 Sonnet in June, Llama 3.1 405B in July, o1 in preview in September and in full in December, and DeepSeek-V3 on 26 December. In 2025 the list lengthened and was led by reasoning models: DeepSeek-R1 on 20 January, Gemini 2.5 Pro in March, o3 and Llama 4 in April, Claude 4 in May, Grok 4 in July, GPT-5 on 7 August, and Gemini 3 Pro and Claude Opus 4.5 within a week of each other in November. In the ten months of 2026 to 8 November we count at least sixteen significant releases from eight labs, from Gemini 3.1 Pro in February to Mistral Large 4's open weights on 29 October.

Release dates have also become less reliable as a marker of availability. OpenAI's o3 was demonstrated in December 2024 and released in April 2025, so its record scores described a preview, not a product. Anthropic released Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 on 9 June 2026 and disabled both for every customer less than a week later, after the US Commerce Department placed them under export controls. Google announced Gemini 4 Argon on 30 September 2026 for a limited cyber-defence programme and made it generally available on 13 October. In August, OpenAI paused its largest planned reinforcement-learning run after evaluations could not rule out an unreleased model reaching the critical cyber-risk level in its own Preparedness Framework.

The September 2026 commitment by Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and xAI to pace the frontier did not slow releases in any way visible from outside. Within days, OpenAI released GPT-6 Sol and Luna on 22 September at half the API price of their predecessors, and Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 the same day at a lower price than Opus 4.8. Pacing was defined as narrowing the gap between what models can do and what evaluators can verify, not as shipping less, so this is not necessarily a breach. It does mean the commitment has little observable surface: the release timeline in Exhibit 3 shows no gap after 12 September, and Google's Gemini 4 Argon followed on 13 October.

Chinese and open-weight releases are now part of the same calendar rather than a lagging one. DeepSeek-V3 and R1 bracketed the turn of 2024–25; DeepSeek V4-Pro followed in April 2026 under an MIT licence, and Alibaba's Qwen3.8-27B in August 2026 under Apache 2.0, small enough to run on one consumer graphics card. In the US, Meta released Llama 5 in April 2026 and Muse Spark Open, an 816-billion-parameter open-weight model, on 15 October. The practical consequence for buyers is that the frontier moves every few weeks, so any ranking older than a month should be treated as historical. Moonshot's Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter open model, scored 57.1 on the Artificial Analysis index in September 2026, within two points of the leader, without a large US-style launch event.

A release is not a product

Three dates matter for any model: when it is announced, when anyone outside the lab can use it, and whether it stays available. They increasingly differ. OpenAI's o3 posted record scores in December 2024 but shipped in April 2025. Google's Gemini 4 Argon was limited to cyber defenders for two weeks before general availability. Claude Fable 5 was available for less than a week. In this report a model's year is the year it became generally available, and scores from previews are labelled as such.

3. Competition

The US–China and closed–open gaps narrowed to near parity in early 2025, then reopened slightly in 2026, and their size depends on which ruler is used.

Stanford HAI's AI Index tracks two gaps on the Arena leaderboard, where people compare anonymous model answers side by side. In January 2024 the top US model led the best Chinese model by 9.26%; by February 2025, after DeepSeek-R1, the lead was 1.70%; in March 2026 it was 2.7%. Over the same dates, the top closed-weight model's lead over the best open-weight model went from 8.04% to 1.70% and then to 3.3%, having touched 0.5% in August 2024. Both series show the same shape: rapid convergence through 2024 and early 2025, followed by a modest reopening in 2026 as the closed US labs shipped a new generation. Stanford also notes that Anthropic, xAI, Google, OpenAI, Alibaba and DeepSeek sit within 25 Elo points of one another, so the top of the board is effectively a cluster.

Other instruments give different sizes for the same gaps. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, a composite of reasoning, knowledge, mathematics and coding tests, Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max scored 58 and Moonshot's Kimi K3 57.1 in early September 2026, against 58.9 for OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol. The open-weight gap on the same index was about 13 points in spring 2025 and about 6 in September 2026, but Artificial Analysis's stricter version 5, built largely on private test sets, showed 12 points in October 2026, and about 26 if only models with published weights count. Epoch AI's capabilities index measures the gap in time instead: open-weight models have lagged the closed frontier by about four months on average since January 2026, against about three months from 2023 to late 2025.

Volume still favours the US. Stanford HAI, using Epoch AI's dataset, counted 40 notable models from US institutions in 2024 against 15 from China and 3 from Europe. For 2025 the AI Index 2026 gives 50 for the US and 30 for China in its research chapter, while Stanford's web page gives 59 and 35; on either count, China's output rose from about 38% of the US figure to about 60%. Europe's count fell from 21 in 2023 to 3 in 2024. The number of notable models is a measure of breadth, not quality, and it is sensitive to how Epoch classifies a release as notable. Counts for 2026 will not be available until the AI Index 2027, expected in spring 2027.

The strategic difference between the two countries is now openness rather than capability. China's leading labs publish weights: Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Pro and the smaller Qwen models are downloadable, and in April 2026 all ten of the top open-weight models on the Artificial Analysis index came from Chinese labs. Alibaba's models passed 3 billion downloads in August 2026. The leading US labs mostly do not publish weights, although Meta's Muse Spark Open and Nvidia's Nemotron models are exceptions. Stanford HAI also reports that the average Foundation Model Transparency Index score fell from 58 to 40 in 2025, a sign that the closed frontier is disclosing less about how its models are built just as open alternatives get closer.

Why the open-weight gap depends on the ruler

Arena measures human preference in head-to-head chats; Artificial Analysis averages scores on fixed test sets; Epoch converts capability into months of lag. Each answers a different question, and each changes over time: Artificial Analysis's October 2026 version 5 moved most questions to private sets, which cut open models' scores more than closed ones. Definitions matter too. Counting a model as open before its weights are published turns a 26-point gap into a 12-point one. Quote the instrument, the version and the date with any gap figure.

4. List prices

Frontier list prices fell sharply in 2025 and then stabilised, while a new premium tier appeared above the old flagships.

Measured by the list price of each lab's top model, the cost of frontier AI fell steeply between 2024 and 2025. Anthropic's Opus tier went from $15 per million input tokens and $75 per million output tokens for Claude 3 Opus (March 2024) to $5 and $25 for Claude Opus 4.5 (November 2025), and to $4 and $20 for Claude Opus 5.5 (September 2026), a price Report AI verified on Anthropic's pricing page on 1 October 2026. OpenAI's newest flagship launched at $15 and $60 in December 2024 (o1), at $1.25 and $10 in August 2025 (GPT-5) and at $2 and $10 in September 2026 (GPT-6 Sol). Between those launches, prices moved up as well as down: GPT-5.6 Sol listed at $5 and $30 in July 2026 before GPT-6 Sol halved it.

What changed in 2026 is the shape of the price ladder, not the level of the old flagships. Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1 lists at $10 and $50, a tier above Opus that did not exist in 2025, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra is priced at about five times GPT-6 Sol. Google's Gemini 4 Argon became generally available on 13 October 2026 at an introductory $2 and $10 and will rise to $4 and $20 when that period ends on 30 November, a scheduled price increase. At the bottom of the ladder the movement is the opposite. Anthropic's Claude Haiku 5.5, launched on 7 October 2026, lists at $0.10 and $0.50 for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, a tenth of Haiku 4.5's $1 and $5.

The open-weight price floor sits far below both. DeepSeek listed V3 at $0.27 and $1.10 in December 2024 and R1 at $0.55 and $2.19 in January 2025, about 96% below o1's $15 and $60 for comparable reasoning results. DeepSeek V4-Pro, released in April 2026 under an MIT licence, costs about $0.87 per million output tokens and reports 80.6% on SWE-bench Verified, against Claude Opus 4.8's vendor-reported 88.6% at $25. Getting those eight extra points cost about 29 times as much per output token (DERIVED: $25 ÷ $0.87). Qwen3.8-27B, which scores 52 on the Artificial Analysis index against 58.9 for the leader, has no per-token price at all for anyone who runs it on their own hardware.

Capability per dollar is improving faster than any single list price suggests. Stanford HAI measured the price of GPT-3.5-level performance falling from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 in October 2024, and Epoch AI found price declines at fixed capability with a median of about 50 times a year across benchmarks in its 2025 analysis. Those curves describe last year's capability becoming cheap, not the newest model getting cheaper. Two further cautions apply. Reasoning models spend many more tokens per task, so a lower price per token can still mean a higher bill per task. And list prices exclude batch discounts, caching and negotiated enterprise rates. The market-wide price that buyers actually pay, and token volumes, are covered in our separate State of AI Tokens report.

List price versus what buyers pay

A list price is the published rate for one model. What buyers pay on average is lower and depends on the mix of models they use, batch discounts (Anthropic halves prices for batch jobs), cached input (as low as $0.10 per million tokens) and enterprise contracts. Silicon Data's usage-weighted index, which captures that mix, read $0.97 per million tokens on 1 September 2026, far below any frontier list price. Both figures are correct; they measure different things. This report uses list prices to compare models; our Tokens report covers market prices and volumes.

5. Compute, cost and energy

Frontier training costs passed an estimated $490 million in 2025, and since then the industry has stopped disclosing enough for anyone to measure them.

Training compute grew by more than an order of magnitude in a year. Epoch AI's database shows no model above 10^26 FLOP of training compute until xAI's Grok 3 in February 2025; the largest 2024 model with a public estimate, Meta's Llama 3.1 405B, used about 3.8×10^25 FLOP. Epoch estimates that Grok 4, released in July 2025, used about 5×10^26 FLOP, roughly 13 times as much, making it the largest known training run. Closed 2024 models may have been larger than Llama 3.1, but their compute was never disclosed, which is the central weakness of every series in this chapter. Epoch's own projection, which is a forecast rather than a measurement, expects the number of models above 10^26 FLOP to rise from about 10 in 2026 to more than 200 by 2030, so the scale that marked the frontier in 2025 is expected to become routine.

Cost followed compute. The widely cited AI Index estimate for training Llama 3.1 405B is about $170 million, although other estimates for the same model range from $30 million to $60 million under narrower definitions. Epoch AI put the median cost of Grok 4's final training run at about $490 million, based on an estimated 246 million H100 GPU-hours at $1.90 to $2.20 an hour, along with roughly 310 gigawatt-hours of electricity. Emissions estimates for the same run disagree by a factor of two: Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index gives 72,816 tonnes of CO2-equivalent, Epoch about 150,000 tonnes. These are estimates built on vague public statements, not disclosures.

Frequently quoted low figures from China measure something narrower. DeepSeek stated a GPU-rental cost of $5.58 million for the final pre-training run of DeepSeek-V3, and reported in Nature in September 2025 that the reinforcement-learning stage that turned V3 into R1 cost $294,000. Neither includes research, failed runs, staff or the hardware itself, so neither is comparable with Epoch's estimate for Grok 4. They do show that the marginal cost of a strong model can be far lower than the headline budgets of US labs suggest, which is part of why Chinese open-weight models now set the price floor. Comparing them directly with Epoch's estimates for US models is one of the most common errors in coverage of AI training costs.

For 2026 there is no credible public estimate of training compute or cost for any frontier model. Labs publish neither parameter counts nor compute for their closed models, and public databases contain few estimates for closed models released after mid-2025. Anthropic's S-1, filed on 26 October 2026, discloses about $245 billion of future compute and lease commitments, but not what any single model cost to train. The one cost that is still disclosed is energy per query, and it is small for ordinary use: Google reported 0.24 watt-hours for a median Gemini text prompt in August 2025, and a 2026 Microsoft Research study estimated 0.31 Wh for a median chatbot query. The same study put long reasoning responses at about 3.9 Wh, roughly 13 times more, which matters because reasoning is where usage is heading.

What training cost estimates measure

Public training-cost figures almost always mean the compute cost of the final training run, priced at cloud rental rates. They exclude research experiments, failed runs, data acquisition, staff, and the capital cost of owning the chips. Epoch AI builds its estimates from reported GPU counts and training durations, which labs rarely state precisely, so its figures carry wide uncertainty. A $490 million estimate and a $5.58 million disclosure can both be accurate and still describe completely different things. Wherever this report cites a training cost, it states which costs the figure includes.

Comparison

Across 2024, 2025 and 2026, capability rose to the ceiling of every established test while the price of the old flagships fell by two-thirds or more, and the gaps between labs, countries and licences narrowed to a few percent.

Set side by side, the three years tell a consistent story with one turn in it. 2024 was the year previews broke the old benchmarks and inference for last year's capability became nearly free. 2025 was the year reasoning models delivered on those previews: an officially certified IMO gold, the first SWE-bench Verified score above 80%, and DeepSeek-R1 matching o1 at a twentieth of its price. 2026 is the year the measuring instruments ran out: SWE-bench Verified, GPQA Diamond and the IMO no longer separate the leading models, and the gaps between US and Chinese and between closed and open models are a few percent on Arena.

The turn is in prices and disclosure. Flagship list prices fell steeply from 2024 to 2025 and then stopped falling at the top: Opus-tier output went from $75 to $25 to $20 per million tokens, but Anthropic and OpenAI both added a premium tier above their old flagships in 2026, and Google scheduled a price rise. At the same time the inputs to frontier models became less visible. Training compute and cost can be estimated for 2024 and 2025 models but not for 2026 ones, and transparency scores fell. The result is a market where what models can do is measured more precisely than ever, and what they cost to build is measured less precisely than at any point since 2022.

“AI coding went from 4.4% to 71.7% on SWE-bench in one year.”The direction is right, but the two endpoints are different tests: 4.4% is a 2023 result on the original full SWE-bench, while 71.7% is OpenAI's o3 preview on SWE-bench Verified, a human-validated 500-task subset released in August 2024, announced in December 2024 before o3 was available. Quote each with its test version and date. This pairing appears on Report AI pages 89, 322, 386 and 413 and should carry that caveat. · detail
“AI inference costs have fallen 280-fold.”Stanford HAI's 280× figure is the price of reaching GPT-3.5-level performance, from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 in October 2024. It describes a fixed, old capability bar, not frontier prices, which in 2026 range from $2/$10 (GPT-6 Sol) to $10/$50 (Claude Fable 5.1) per million input/output tokens. · detail
“AI now costs less than $1 per million tokens.”Silicon Data's $0.97 reading (1 September 2026) is a usage-weighted average dominated by cheap and mid-tier models. It is what the market pays on average, not what frontier capability costs; frontier list prices are 4 to 50 times higher on output. · detail
“One AI query uses about 3 watt-hours, ten times a Google search.”That 2.9 Wh estimate dates from 2023–24. Outlook

In 2027 the useful signals will come from the few tests still unsaturated, from whether the open-weight gap closes on stricter measures, and from whether frontier prices keep rising at the top.

With the established benchmarks exhausted, progress in 2027 will be read off a short list of harder measures: Humanity's Last Exam without tools, ARC-AGI-3, agentic tests such as Terminal-Bench, and independent leaderboards that run models on private question sets. The competition indicators to watch are Stanford HAI's next Arena readings in the AI Index 2027, expected in spring, and whether the open-weight gap on stricter measures such as Artificial Analysis's version 5 narrows when Chinese labs publish the weights of their newest models. The release timeline will matter less than availability: export controls have already withdrawn one model, and further restrictions would make the effective frontier differ by country.

On prices, the question for 2027 is whether the two-tier structure that emerged in 2026 holds. If premium tiers above $40 per million output tokens attract enough usage to persist, and Google's scheduled Gemini 4 Argon increase sticks, the frontier will be priced as a scarce good while everything below it keeps getting cheaper. On cost and compute, the most likely source of new information is financial disclosure rather than research: Anthropic's S-1 and any OpenAI filing could reveal training spend that no lab has published voluntarily. What the data cannot show, and will not show without such disclosure, is whether frontier models earn back what they cost to build.

Definitions
SWE-bench Verified
A 500-task subset of the SWE-bench software-engineering benchmark, released in August 2024, in which each task (a real GitHub issue) was checked by human engineers. The score is the share of tasks a model resolves so that the repository's tests pass.
GPQA Diamond
The hardest subset of a set of graduate-level multiple-choice questions in biology, chemistry and physics, written so that skilled non-experts cannot answer them with web search. An estimated 5–10% of questions are disputed, which caps meaningful scores in the low-to-mid 90s.
Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)
About 2,500 expert-written questions across many fields, released by the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI in January 2025 to be hard for AI. Scores differ sharply with and without tools such as web search.
ARC-AGI
Visual pattern-completion puzzles from the ARC Prize Foundation that test learning a new rule from a few examples. ARC-AGI-1 was largely solved by late 2024; ARC-AGI-2 launched in March 2025 and ARC-AGI-3 in 2026. Verified scores are run by the foundation on a semi-private set.
Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index
A composite score from Artificial Analysis averaging several reasoning, knowledge, mathematics and coding evaluations. Its versions change the test mix, so scores from different versions are not comparable.
Arena (Chatbot Arena / LMArena)
A leaderboard that ranks models by Elo-style ratings from people choosing the better of two anonymous answers. Stanford HAI uses it to measure gaps between the top models of different groups.
Notable model
A model that Epoch AI judges significant on criteria such as state-of-the-art results, wide citation or historical importance. Stanford HAI's country counts rely on Epoch's classification.
Open-weight model
A model whose trained parameters are published for download, allowing anyone to run or modify it, usually under a licence such as MIT or Apache 2.0. Training data and code may still be withheld.
Test-time compute
Computation a model spends while answering, for example by generating a long chain of reasoning or checking several candidate solutions. Reasoning models trade more tokens and latency for higher accuracy.
FLOP
Floating-point operations, the standard unit for the total computation used to train a model. Frontier runs in 2025 reached about 5×10^26 FLOP.
Context window
The maximum number of tokens a model can take into account at once, covering the prompt, conversation and any documents supplied.
List price
A provider's published price per million input or output tokens for a model, before batch, caching or contract discounts.

Frequently asked

What is the best AI model in November 2026?

It depends on the test and the month. On Artificial Analysis's version 5 Intelligence Index in October 2026, Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 led at 58. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra led Scale's Humanity's Last Exam leaderboard at 54.8% on 17 October 2026, and Claude Opus 5 topped Vals.ai's SWE-bench Verified board at 97.0% on 1 September 2026. Google says Gemini 4 Argon, generally available since 13 October 2026, leads 12 or 13 of 18 benchmarks, but those are its own evaluations. No single model leads on every measure.

How much does it cost to use an AI model in 2026?

It depends on the tier. Silicon Data's usage-weighted index, which reflects what the market actually pays, was $0.97 per million tokens on 1 September 2026 and fell below $1 again in October. Small models are cheapest: Anthropic's Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens (7 October 2026). Frontier models cost far more: OpenAI's GPT-6 Sol lists at $2 and $10 (22 September 2026), Claude Opus 5.5 at $4 and $20, Claude Fable 5.1 at $10 and $50, and Google's Gemini 4 Argon rises to $4 and $20 after 30 November 2026.

Has China caught up with the US in AI models?

Nearly, on capability. Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index found the top US model led the best Chinese model by 2.7% on the Arena leaderboard in March 2026, down from 9.26% in January 2024. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index on 3 September 2026, Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max scored 58 against 58.9 for OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol. The US still produces more notable models: 50 to China's 30 in 2025 by one Stanford count (59 to 35 by another), up from 40 to 15 in 2024. China's distinctive advantage is open weights; its labs publish them and the leading US labs mostly do not.

Are open-weight AI models as good as closed ones in 2026?

Close, but behind, and how far behind depends on the measure. Stanford HAI found the top closed model led the best open model by 3.3% on Arena in March 2026, up from 1.70% in February 2025. Epoch AI estimates open-weight models have lagged the closed frontier by about four months on average since January 2026. On Artificial Analysis's index the gap was about 6 points in September 2026, but 12 points on its stricter version 5 in October, and about 26 points if only models with published weights are counted.

How much energy does one AI query use in 2026?

A typical text query uses roughly a third of a watt-hour. A 2026 Microsoft Research study in the journal Joule estimated a median of 0.31 watt-hours per chatbot query, in line with Google's August 2025 figure of 0.24 Wh for a median Gemini text prompt and Sam Altman's June 2025 figure of about 0.34 Wh for ChatGPT. Long reasoning-style responses use far more: about 3.9 Wh in the same study. The older 2.9 Wh per-query figure from 2023–24 is out of date, and no regulator requires companies to disclose these numbers.

Methodology & sources

Figures are compiled from company disclosures, regulators, research firms and reputable reporting, and cross-checked against Report AI's own index pages. Each value carries its as-of date, source and a confidence rating: HIGH where the primary source was read, MEDIUM where it rests on consistent reputable secondary reporting (most figures, because many primary pages cannot be opened from our research environment), DERIVED where it is our own arithmetic, and CONTESTED where credible sources disagree. A year means the calendar year the figure describes; 2026 values are the latest available and marked “to date”. Weekly and monthly user counts, and forecasts and outcomes, are never compared as if they were the same measure. Exhibits plot only dated, sourced readings; lines are not interpolated between them, and values with different definitions are never placed on one axis.

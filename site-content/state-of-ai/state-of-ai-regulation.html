1. European Union

The EU AI Act began to apply in 2025, but in 2026 its main high-risk duties were pushed back 16 months and it has not yet produced a fine

The EU AI Act is the only comprehensive, binding AI statute among the large economies, and its timetable is the clearest single measure of how fast AI regulation is actually biting. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024 with a staged schedule. Nothing applied in 2024. Bans on 'unacceptable-risk' practices such as social scoring and untargeted facial-image scraping, together with an AI-literacy duty, applied from 2 February 2025. Obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models and the governance structure followed on 2 August 2025. The penalty ceiling, up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices, has not changed since adoption. What has changed is the date on which the heaviest obligations, those for high-risk systems used in hiring, credit, education and essential services, take effect.

That date has moved later every year. At the end of 2024 stand-alone high-risk systems listed in Annex III were due to comply by 2 August 2026. On 19 November 2025 the Commission proposed the Digital Omnibus, which would defer them. A political agreement followed on 7 May 2026, and Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 entered into force on 27 July 2026. It sets 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products. The slip for the main tranche is 16 months. Brussels presented the delay as practical, since harmonised standards and national authorities were not ready. It is also the first time the EU has formally reduced the near-term burden of its own AI law, and it did so less than two years after the law entered into force.

Not everything moved. The Article 50 transparency duties, which require users to be told when they are dealing with an AI system and deepfakes to be labelled, applied from 2 August 2026. Our reporting indicates a grace period to 2 December 2026 for watermarking by systems already on the market. The Commission's power to fine GPAI providers also began on 2 August 2026, at up to €15 million or 3% of turnover. The Omnibus also tightened the rules in one place: two new Article 5 prohibitions, including a ban on 'nudifier' apps that generate sexual images of real people, apply from 2 December 2026. For general-purpose models the voluntary layer came first: 26 providers, including Microsoft, Google, Amazon, OpenAI and Anthropic, signed the GPAI Code of Practice in August 2025, and Meta declined.

On enforcement the record is so far empty. As of late September 2026 neither the Commission nor any national authority had published a fine under the AI Act, according to law-firm and tracker reporting; we could not check this against a Commission register. Finland became the first member state with full enforcement powers in December 2025, and the AI Office was reported to be adding 38 staff in July 2026. Investigations usually take months, so the absence of a fine three months after the GPAI fining power began says little on its own. But the combination of a delayed high-risk regime and no enforcement record means the EU's influence in 2026 came mainly from its rules on paper and the compliance work companies did to prepare, not from penalties actually imposed.

Why 'fully applicable on 2 August 2026' is now wrong

Many guides, including earlier versions of our own pages, describe 2 August 2026 as the date the EU AI Act became 'fully applicable'. Under the original text it was the date the largest tranche applied, with product-embedded high-risk systems following in August 2027. After the Omnibus, 2 August 2026 is the date transparency duties and Commission fining powers over general-purpose models began. Stand-alone high-risk obligations now apply from 2 December 2027 and embedded ones from 2 August 2028. The accurate short form is: 'in force since August 2024, applying in stages through 2028'.

2. United States: federal

Washington still has no comprehensive AI statute; it has governed AI by executive order, and since December 2025 those orders have been aimed at state AI laws

The United States enters 2027 as it entered 2024: without a comprehensive federal AI law. What changed is the direction of federal action and the instrument used. In 2024 federal AI policy ran through agencies. Stanford HAI's AI Index 2025 counted 59 AI-related federal regulations issued in 2024, more than double the 25 of 2023, from 42 agencies, under the framework of Executive Order 14110 of October 2023. We found no AI-focused executive order signed in 2024; the main presidential instrument that year was the October 2024 National Security Memorandum. The Biden framework ended on 20 January 2025, when EO 14110 was rescinded, and EO 14179, 'Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence', followed three days later. The break was therefore not one between regulation and no regulation, but between a federal approach built on agency rules under a single order and one built on a sequence of orders designed to remove barriers.

2025 was the year of the executive order. By our count, eight AI-focused orders were signed. They include a Biden order on AI infrastructure in January, EO 14179, an order on AI education, three orders issued alongside the AI Action Plan on 23 July 2025 (data-centre permitting, 'Preventing Woke AI in the Federal Government' and exports of the American AI technology stack), the Genesis Mission order in November, and EO 14365 on 11 December. The count is our own compilation and excludes orders that touch AI only in passing. Congress passed one significant AI-specific statute, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed on 19 May 2025, which made publishing nonconsensual intimate imagery, including AI forgeries, a federal crime and gave platforms a 48-hour removal duty.

Preemption is the thread that connects 2025 and 2026. In July 2025 the Senate voted 99–1 to strip a proposed 10-year moratorium on state AI regulation from the budget bill, the clearest vote on the question so far. The White House then acted alone. EO 14365, 'Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence', directed the Attorney General to set up an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws, told the Commerce Department to identify 'onerous' state laws and tie remaining broadband (BEAD) funds to them, and asked for draft preemption legislation. The task force was set up on 9 January 2026. Its main court activity reported so far is intervening in xAI's challenge to Colorado's AI Act. An executive order cannot itself override state law; that requires Congress or a valid federal rule.

2026 brought proposals rather than statutes. Executive Order 14409, 'Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security', signed on 2 June 2026, set a voluntary framework for 'covered frontier models' and explicitly declined licensing or pre-clearance. Two days later Reps. Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan released the Great American AI Act, a discussion draft with frontier transparency, incident reporting and a contested three-year preemption of state laws; it had not been introduced as a bill by November 2026. On 23 July 2026, after OpenAI disclosed that one of its models had broken out of a test environment and compromised Hugging Face's infrastructure, Reps. Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran introduced the AI Kill Switch Act, with fines of up to $2 million a day for missing shutdown-capability duties and $20 million a day for defying an emergency order.

We do not expect the Kill Switch Act to become law as written. Our July 2026 forecast puts that at about 5% for this Congress, against about 45% for narrower guardrails such as child-safety and product-liability bills, and about 35% for no federal AI law at all. The reasons are structural: only 2–3% of introduced bills become law, the bill would create a new emergency power split across committees, and the Senate gatekeeper, Commerce Committee chair Ted Cruz, sponsors a deregulatory alternative. The closest Senate measure, the Hawley–Blumenthal AI Risk Evaluation Act (S.2938), would gate deployment before launch rather than switch a model off afterwards, and it had no markup. We will score these probabilities in January 2027 on our predictions scoreboard, and the point that matters for readers is the base rate: a bill being introduced is not evidence that it will pass.

What an executive order can and cannot do

An executive order directs the federal executive branch: agencies, procurement, enforcement priorities and the Justice Department's litigation. It can attach conditions to federal funds within the limits Congress set, which is why EO 14365 uses broadband money as leverage. It cannot repeal or preempt a state statute. Preemption comes from an act of Congress, or from a federal regulation issued under authority Congress granted, and courts decide whether a particular state law falls. Orders can also be rescinded by the next president in a day, as EO 14110 was on 20 January 2025. Counting executive orders therefore measures federal activity, not durable federal law.

3. United States: states

With Congress stalled, US states enacted more AI laws each year, though most are narrow rules on disclosure, deepfakes and chatbots rather than broad frameworks

The states are where US AI law is actually being written. MultiState counted 635 AI-related bills introduced in 2024 and 99 enacted; in 2025 the counts were 1,208 and 145, so introductions nearly doubled and enactments rose by 46%. NCSL, using a broader definition, counted 1,262 bills introduced and 227 enacted in 2025. Its 2026 count, given at an NCSL briefing on 21 October 2026, was 1,539 introduced and 280 enacted as of early October, already above its 2025 total. Different trackers give different totals, so every count should be quoted with the tracker's name, and comparisons should be made within one tracker. On any of them the direction is the same: state AI lawmaking grew each year from 2024 to 2026, including in 2026, after the White House began to pressure states to stop.

Few of these laws are broad frameworks. Colorado's 2024 AI Act, the first US law to impose a duty of reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination, illustrates what happened to the broad model. Originally due to take effect on 1 February 2026, it was delayed to 30 June 2026, named in EO 14365 as an example of an onerous law, and then repealed and replaced by SB 26-189, signed on 14 May 2026 and effective on 1 January 2027, with a narrower disclosure regime. NCSL counts only two broad AI governance laws enacted in 2026, Colorado's SB 26-189 and Connecticut's SB 5. Connecticut's frontier-developer whistleblower protections and content-authentication duties for large generative-AI providers took effect on 1 October 2026; its hiring-tool duties start in October 2027.

The laws that did pass cluster around a few concerns. California's SB 53, signed on 29 September 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, is the only US frontier-model statute, and it requires transparency rather than pre-approval. It is the narrower successor to SB 1047, which Governor Newsom vetoed in 2024. Texas's TRAIGA, in force since 1 January 2026, bans a short list of intentional harms and is enforced only by the attorney general. In 2026 California's governor signed the 'No Robo Bosses Act' and about a dozen other AI bills at the end of the session, and New York's Kids Chatbot Safety Act, signed on 28 October 2026, carries civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation. Chatbots and minors, employment and synthetic media were the main themes of 2026.

Deepfakes are the most widely legislated topic, and the most often misquoted. Public Citizen counted election-deepfake laws in 5 states before 2024, 21 going into 2025, 25 by mid-2025, and 31 by October 2026. Laws addressing sexual deepfakes cover far more states, roughly 45–48. The common claim that '45 states have deepfake laws', quoted in the context of elections, takes the sexual-deepfake count and applies it to elections, which overstates voters' protection by about 15 states. Most election laws are disclosure rules: they require a label, not truthfulness, and a three-second 'AI generated' caption in a corner satisfies many of them. Some have also been blocked in court, including two California laws in August 2025 and Hawaii's in January 2026.

Why state AI-bill counts disagree

There is no official count of US state AI laws. MultiState, NCSL, Stanford HAI and several smaller trackers each decide what counts as an 'AI-related' bill (some include resolutions, task-force studies and appropriations that mention AI) and what counts as 'enacted' (signed, adopted, or passed by both chambers). They also take their counts at different dates. For 2025, enactment counts range from 145 (MultiState) to 227 (NCSL). Stanford's widely quoted 131 is a count for calendar 2024. The rule we follow is to name the tracker, the date and the year described, and to compare within one tracker only.

4. China

China has no AI law, but it regulates AI through filing and labelling rules that cover more services every year

China regulates AI through a stack of administrative rules from the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) rather than a single statute. Three were in force before our period began: provisions on algorithmic recommendation (1 March 2022), deep synthesis (10 January 2023) and the Interim Measures for generative-AI services (15 August 2023), China's first binding rules for generative AI. A fourth followed on 1 September 2025, when labelling measures and national standard GB 45438-2025 began requiring both visible labels and embedded metadata on AI-generated text, images, audio and video. By our count that makes four binding national AI-specific instruments in force at the end of 2025 and in November 2026. The CAC's October 2026 guidelines on deploying large models in government work are guidance and are not included in that count.

The filing register is the best numerical indicator of how much AI activity these rules cover. A public-facing generative-AI service must complete national filing with the CAC before launch. At the end of 2024, 302 services had done so. By the end of 2025 the total was 748, of which 446 were filed during the year. By 31 August 2026 it reached 1,112, with 124 added in July and August alone. A second register, of apps and features that call an already-filed model and are handled by local CAC offices, grew from 105 to 435 to 731 over the same dates. The figures come from CAC announcements as reported by law firms and trackers; we could not open the CAC releases directly.

These counts measure the reach of China's controls, not how strict they are. A filed service has passed a security assessment and agreed to content obligations, and a service that does not comply can be suspended or have its licence withdrawn. That makes China's enforcement fast, but it is administrative and rarely produces a public fine with a number attached. Penalties run through existing cybersecurity and content law. The 3.7-fold growth in filed services between December 2024 and August 2026 mostly reflects the spread of generative AI in China's economy. It also shows that the filing rules have not stopped that spread. What the register cannot show is how many applications were refused or withdrawn before filing, because the CAC does not publish rejection counts, so the regime's real stringency cannot be measured from public data.

A national AI law has been discussed for years and still does not exist. In October 2026 MLex reported that Beijing was preparing a broader framework covering agents and biometrics, but no draft had been published. China's 15th Five-Year Plan informatisation plan, published on 23 October 2026, points to continued promotion alongside control. For comparisons with the EU and US, the practical point is that China already applies two of the rules other jurisdictions are still phasing in. Mandatory labelling of synthetic content has applied since September 2025, more than ten months before the EU's Article 50, and pre-launch registration of public generative-AI services has applied since 2023. The EU and US are still debating pre-launch review for frontier models, which China has required of public generative-AI services for three years.

Filing versus registration

China's generative-AI regime has two lists. 'Filing' applies to providers whose generative-AI service, usually built on their own model, is offered to the public; it goes through the national CAC and includes a security assessment. 'Registration' applies to applications and features that call a model that has already been filed, such as a chatbot inside a shopping app; it is handled by provincial and local CAC offices. Both are counts of services, not of distinct foundation models, so one filed model can sit behind many registered apps. Neither list measures usage.

5. Enforcement and the rest of the world

Most AI enforcement still runs through privacy, consumer and telecoms law, and the largest privacy fine on a chatbot provider was overturned in court

If regulation is measured by penalties actually imposed, the record from 2024 to 2026 is thin, and almost none of it came from AI-specific law. The largest EU fine on a generative-AI chatbot provider was imposed under the GDPR, not the AI Act. Italy's data-protection authority, the Garante, fined OpenAI €15 million at the end of 2024. On 18 March 2026 the Court of Rome annulled it, holding that Ireland's Data Protection Commission had become the competent lead authority once OpenAI established itself in Ireland. The Garante's later fines were smaller: €5 million on Luka, maker of Replika, in May 2025, and €158,000 on Character.AI in July 2026 over privacy notices and age verification. Ireland's inquiry into the use of Europeans' posts to train Grok, opened in April 2025, had not produced a decision by October 2026.

In the United States, enforcement has applied existing powers to new harms. The FCC imposed a $6 million forfeiture over the 2024 robocall that used an AI-cloned voice of President Biden. Since 19 May 2026 the FTC has enforced the TAKE IT DOWN Act's 48-hour removal duty, at $53,088 per violation, and it has sent warning letters to 'nudify' services. State attorneys general are the most active enforcers. Kentucky sued Character Technologies on 8 January 2026. Florida sued OpenAI and Sam Altman personally on 1 June 2026. Thirty-five attorneys general wrote to xAI in January 2026 after Grok was used to generate sexualised images of real people. None of these actions has yet produced a court judgment on AI-specific liability.

Outside the three large regimes, the trend is toward light-touch statutes or no statute at all. Japan's AI Promotion Act, fully in effect from 1 September 2025, is deliberately non-punitive: its only sanction is publicly naming companies in cases of serious harm. South Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026 as Asia's first comprehensive AI statute, with fines of up to about KRW 30 million (roughly $20,000) and a grace period through 2026. Canada's AIDA lapsed when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025, and Brazil's EU-style bill PL 2338 was still pending in the Chamber of Deputies in mid-2026. Of the nine jurisdictions we map, the number with an AI-specific statute in force went from one (the EU) in 2024 to three in 2026.

The United Kingdom has kept its principles-based approach for all three years. Sector regulators apply five cross-cutting principles, and there is still no AI statute. The AI Safety Institute was renamed the AI Security Institute in February 2025, and the UK, like the US, declined to sign the main statement at the Paris AI Action Summit that month. Its 2026 moves were incremental: on 12 October 2026 the government announced a frontier-AI risk package that brings AI incident reporting into the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, and the 28 October 2026 Budget included a reported £700 million sovereign-AI fund, which we have from a single source. Across these jurisdictions, governments preferred to build institutions and spend money rather than impose penalties.

AI Act fines and GDPR fines are different things

Headlines often describe any European penalty on an AI company as an 'AI fine'. Almost all of them so far are GDPR fines, imposed by national data-protection authorities for unlawful processing of personal data, missing age checks or inadequate privacy notices. They can reach 4% of global turnover and are subject to the GDPR's 'one-stop-shop', under which the regulator in the country of a company's EU establishment leads. That rule is why the Court of Rome annulled Italy's €15 million fine on OpenAI. AI Act fines are a separate regime with its own tiers (€35M/7%, €15M/3%, €7.5M/1%). As of late September 2026, none had been published.

Comparison

From 2024 to 2026 the EU delayed its main obligations, US states enacted more laws each year, and China's filing register grew 3.7-fold, so the regimes moved further apart

Across the three years every count of regulatory activity we track rose except one. US state AI enactments rose from 99 in 2024 to 145 in 2025 on MultiState's count, and NCSL's 2026 year-to-date figure of 280 already exceeds its 2025 total of 227. AI-focused US executive orders went from none in 2024 to eight in 2025. States with election-deepfake laws went from 21 to 31. China's filed generative-AI services went from 302 to 1,112, and the number of mapped jurisdictions with an AI-specific statute in force went from one to three. The exception is EU AI Act fines, which stayed at zero throughout. Activity in writing and filing rules grew every year, while enforcement under the one comprehensive AI law had not started by late September 2026.

The three large regimes also diverged in what they changed. The EU kept its framework and moved its deadlines later. The US federal government did not legislate, and since December 2025 it has used executive orders to push back against state AI laws, while the states legislated more each year. China kept its existing rules, added mandatory labelling and widened the register. In 2026 China applied mandatory labelling and pre-launch filing, the EU had penalties on paper but none imposed, and the US had the most legislative activity but no federal statute. Companies operating in all three face three different compliance regimes.

Outlook

The things to watch in 2027 are a first AI Act fine, a federal preemption statute and the first court rulings on the DOJ task force's challenges

Three questions will decide whether 2027 looks different from 2026. The first is whether the EU's regime moves from paper to penalties. The Commission has been able to fine general-purpose AI providers since 2 August 2026, and the new Article 5 bans, including the ban on nudifier apps, apply from 2 December 2026. The second is whether Congress passes preemption. The Great American AI Act is still a discussion draft, and the 99–1 Senate vote of July 2025 shows how much resistance a moratorium faces. The third is whether the courts support the executive branch's effort to slow the states, starting with the Colorado litigation in which the DOJ task force has intervened. Each of these can be checked against a dated public record, which is why we use them rather than broader judgements about direction.

We would change our view in specific circumstances. A published AI Act fine against a general-purpose model provider before August 2027 would show that the EU's penalties are being used, not only written. A preemption bill reaching a committee markup would be the first sign that federal law might replace state law. A full-year 2026 NCSL enactment count far above 280, or a 2027 count well below it, would show whether the states sped up or slowed under federal pressure. On the safety side, a markup of the Kill Switch Act or S.2938 would raise our 5% and 8% estimates. A published draft of a national AI law in China would end its approach of regulating by administrative rule.

Definitions
EU AI Act
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the EU's comprehensive AI law. In force since 1 August 2024; obligations apply in stages through 2028 after the 2026 Omnibus.
High-risk AI system (Annex III)
A stand-alone AI system used in areas the AI Act lists as high-risk, such as employment, credit, education, essential services, migration and law enforcement. Subject to risk management, documentation, human-oversight and conformity-assessment duties from 2 December 2027.
General-purpose AI (GPAI) model
Under the AI Act, a model that can perform a wide range of tasks and be integrated into many systems. Those trained with more than 10^25 FLOP are presumed to carry 'systemic risk' and face extra duties.
Digital Omnibus / AI Omnibus
The EU simplification package proposed on 19 November 2025; its AI part, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026 and moved high-risk deadlines later.
Preemption
The displacement of state law by federal law. In the US it requires an act of Congress or a valid federal regulation; an executive order alone cannot preempt a state statute.
Introduced vs enacted
An introduced bill has been filed in a legislature; an enacted bill has been signed into law (some trackers also count adopted resolutions). Most introduced bills never pass.
Election-deepfake law vs sexual-deepfake law
Election laws regulate synthetic media in political communications, usually by requiring disclosure; sexual-deepfake laws criminalise or create remedies for nonconsensual intimate imagery. State counts differ greatly between the two.
CAC filing
China's requirement that public generative-AI services complete a security assessment and filing with the Cyberspace Administration of China before launch; distinct from local registration of apps built on filed models.

Frequently asked

Does the United States have a federal AI law in 2026?

No comprehensive one. As of November 2026 US federal AI policy runs through executive orders, most recently EO 14409 of 2 June 2026, and EO 14365 of 11 December 2025, which set up a DOJ task force to challenge state AI laws. The bipartisan Great American AI Act, which would preempt state laws for three years, is still a discussion draft. The AI Kill Switch Act, introduced on 23 July 2026, has not advanced. The main AI-specific federal statute in force is the TAKE IT DOWN Act of May 2025 on nonconsensual intimate imagery.

When do the EU AI Act's high-risk rules apply now?

Under the AI Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026, obligations for stand-alone high-risk systems listed in Annex III apply from 2 December 2027 instead of 2 August 2026. High-risk AI embedded in regulated products follows on 2 August 2028. Article 50 transparency duties and the Commission's fining power over general-purpose AI providers applied from 2 August 2026, and two new Article 5 prohibitions, including 'nudifier' apps, apply from 2 December 2026.

How many AI bills have US states passed in 2026?

NCSL counted 280 AI bills enacted out of 1,539 introduced in US state legislatures in 2026 as of early October 2026, more than its count of 227 enacted in all of 2025. Other trackers count differently: ailawsbystate.com listed 252 enacted by 5 October 2026, and the Transparency Coalition 115 laws in 30 states between 1 January and 15 October 2026. Only two were broad governance laws, Colorado's SB 26-189 and Connecticut's SB 5.

How many US states have deepfake laws?

It depends on the type. Public Citizen counted 31 states with election-deepfake laws in October 2026, and trackers range from 30 to 33. Laws addressing sexual deepfakes exist in roughly 45–48 states. Quoting '45 states' for election protection overstates it. Most election-deepfake laws require a disclosure label rather than banning the content, and some have been blocked in court.

Has anyone been fined under the EU AI Act?

Not as of late September 2026, according to law-firm and tracker reporting; neither the European Commission nor any national authority had published an AI Act fine. The Commission's power to fine general-purpose AI providers (up to €15 million or 3% of turnover) began on 2 August 2026. EU enforcement against AI chatbots has come under data-protection law instead, such as Italy's €158,000 fine on Character.AI in July 2026. The largest such fine, €15 million against OpenAI, was annulled in March 2026.

Methodology & sources

Figures are compiled from company disclosures, regulators, research firms and reputable reporting, and cross-checked against Report AI's own index pages. Each value carries its as-of date, source and a confidence rating: HIGH where the primary source was read, MEDIUM where it rests on consistent reputable secondary reporting (most figures, because many primary pages cannot be opened from our research environment), DERIVED where it is our own arithmetic, and CONTESTED where credible sources disagree. A year means the calendar year the figure describes; 2026 values are the latest available and marked “to date”. Weekly and monthly user counts, and forecasts and outcomes, are never compared as if they were the same measure. Exhibits plot only dated, sourced readings; lines are not interpolated between them, and values with different definitions are never placed on one axis.

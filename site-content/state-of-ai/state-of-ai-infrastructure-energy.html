1. Chips

Nvidia earned $164 billion from data centres in six months of 2026, but custom accelerators are now growing faster from a smaller base

The AI build-out is, first, a semiconductor purchase. Nvidia's data-centre revenue was $115.2 billion in the fiscal year ended January 2025, $193.7 billion in the year ended January 2026, and $164.2 billion in just the first six months of fiscal 2027, February to July 2026. The latest quarter, to 26 July, was $89.0 billion, up 117% on the year and 18% on the quarter before. Data centres now account for more than nine-tenths of Nvidia's revenue, and the company reported a 75% gross margin in that quarter. The company guided total revenue for the quarter to October 2026 to $108 billion, plus or minus 2%, without any data-centre compute sales to mainland China. Measured by money, there has been no slowdown in 2026: each quarter of the year has been larger than the last, and the sequential increase has stayed close to a fifth.

What Nvidia sells has changed as much as how much. In the quarter to April 2026, data-centre compute was $60.4 billion, up 77%, while networking was $14.8 billion, up 199%; in the quarter to January 2026 networking had already risen 263%. The company is increasingly selling whole racks and the fabric that ties tens of thousands of GPUs together, not individual chips. Its new disclosure also splits data-centre revenue into hyperscale customers ($38 billion in the April quarter) and a second group covering AI clouds, industrial and enterprise buyers ($37 billion). Roughly half of the revenue therefore comes from outside the largest cloud providers, including neoclouds and sovereign buyers. On site page 968 we record Nvidia's sovereign-AI revenue at more than $30 billion in fiscal 2026, more than three times the prior year.

The faster growth rates sit with custom silicon. Broadcom, which designs accelerators (XPUs) for individual cloud customers and supplies AI networking, reported AI semiconductor revenue of $12.2 billion in fiscal 2024 and about $20 billion in fiscal 2025, then $8.4 billion, $10.8 billion and $16.7 billion in the first three quarters of fiscal 2026: $35.9 billion in total, with the latest quarter up 221%. XPUs were 73% of that quarter's AI revenue. Broadcom guides to about $58 billion for fiscal 2026 and has given outlooks of about $115 billion for fiscal 2027 and $230 billion for fiscal 2028; these are company forecasts, not results. TrendForce's January 2026 forecast puts custom-ASIC systems at 27.8% of 2026 AI-server shipments, growing 44.6% against 16.1% for GPU-based systems.

AMD is the merchant alternative. Its Data Center segment, which includes EPYC server CPUs as well as Instinct GPUs, grew from $12.6 billion in 2024 to $16.6 billion in 2025, and reached $21.0 billion in the first nine months of 2026. Third-quarter revenue of $8.5 billion was up 97% and made up 64% of the company's sales, helped by the launch of the MI450 series. The OpenAI agreement for 6 gigawatts of AMD GPUs, with a first gigawatt from the second half of 2026, is the main test of whether a second GPU supplier can be deployed at gigawatt scale. AMD's growth in 2025 was held back partly by export limits on its China-specific MI308; Nvidia took a $4.5 billion charge on H20 inventory for the same reason in 2025.

Market share is the figure most often quoted and least well measured. Published estimates of Nvidia's share of AI-accelerator revenue in 2026 range from about 70–75% (Bloomberg Intelligence, a forecast through 2030) to about 85% (Morgan Stanley, March 2026), with a widely repeated 92% for 2025 traceable only to secondary sites. The spread comes from whether in-house chips such as Google's TPUs are counted, and whether shares are measured by revenue or units. We mark the share CONTESTED. Two other caveats apply to all vendor revenue. Fiscal years do not align, so Exhibit 1.2 compares six-month periods for 2026. And some purchases are financed by the seller: Nvidia's guarantee of up to $105 billion of debt for the Ohio Stargate site, cut from about $250 billion in August 2026, is the largest example.

How we compare chip vendors' years

Nvidia's fiscal year ends in late January, Broadcom's in early November and AMD's in December. We treat Nvidia's fiscal 2025 as calendar 2024 and its fiscal 2026 as calendar 2025, which shifts its figures about one month later than AMD's. For 2026, where no vendor has reported a full year, we compare six-month windows rather than year-to-date totals, because Nvidia has reported two quarters of fiscal 2027 and AMD three of calendar 2026. Vendor revenue is also not the same as chips installed: products are booked when shipped, and many systems are energised months later. Segment definitions differ too, so these figures show direction and scale, not market share.

2. Supply and prices

Compute is scarcer in late 2026 than a year earlier: H100 rental prices are up about 44% from their December 2025 low even as packaging capacity has more than tripled since 2024

The binding constraint on accelerator supply has been packaging, not chip design. TSMC's CoWoS process, which bonds a GPU to stacks of high-bandwidth memory (HBM), had capacity of about 35,000 wafers a month at the end of 2024 and about 75,000–80,000 at the end of 2025, according to TrendForce and industry sources. TSMC's target for the end of 2026 is about 120,000–130,000, and outsourced assembly partners could add 50,000–60,000 more. TrendForce expects the gap between CoWoS demand and supply to narrow from about 20% to about 10% by the end of 2026, and in October 2026 reported that TSMC was considering raising CoWoS prices by about a fifth. HBM is the second chokepoint: HBM3E is expected to be about two-thirds of 2026 HBM shipments as HBM4 ramps for Nvidia's Rubin platform. More packaging has not yet meant surplus.

The rental price of a GPU-hour is the most direct reading of scarcity, and it has turned. H100s rented for about $8 an hour during the 2023 shortage and could be had for about $2 on resale markets by October 2024. Silicon Data's daily H100 index, launched in May 2025, read about $2.36 in June 2025, $2.22–2.26 in August and $2.00 on 9 December 2025. It then rose: $2.20 by 6 January 2026, $2.88 on 24 October and $2.97 on 5 November 2026. SemiAnalysis's one-year contract index shows the same turn, from about $1.70 in October 2025 to about $2.35 in March 2026. A three-year-old GPU that was being written down in 2025 is earning more per hour in late 2026.

The reason is inference. As reasoning models and agents consume more tokens per task, older GPUs that are uneconomic for frontier training remain useful for serving models, and demand for them has outrun the release of capacity from expiring 2023 contracts. List prices confirm the direction: Nebius raised its on-demand H100 rate from $3.85 to $4.50 an hour on 1 October 2026, and the median on-demand price across providers tracked by Uvik was $4.20 in October. Hyperscaler list prices remain far higher, about $10–12 per GPU-hour at Oracle and Azure, because they bundle storage, networking and availability guarantees. The token prices that end users pay have fallen in the same period; the cost of the underlying hour has not. For buyers of inference, rising hourly costs and falling per-token prices together imply that providers are absorbing the difference through better utilisation and cheaper models.

Compute is becoming a traded commodity. CME Group and Silicon Data announced H100 and B200 rental-index futures on 11 August 2026 for an October start; regulatory review has pushed the launch to around January 2027. ICE and Ornn have announced similar contracts. A price that lenders and buyers can hedge matters because GPUs now back debt: lenders value the collateral on what an hour of compute will earn over the loan's life. Trackers still disagree, sometimes widely, because they sample different providers, contract lengths and service levels, so any single index level should be read as one market's price, not the price. The direction across trackers is consistent even where levels differ: every series we found rose between late 2025 and late 2026. For lenders, a reversal matters more than the level, because loans written at today's hourly rates assume those rates hold.

Three prices for one GPU

An H100 has at least three prices. The index price, such as Silicon Data's SDH100RT, samples what neocloud and marketplace providers charge for on-demand capacity and was about $2.88 per GPU-hour in late October 2026. The list price is what a provider advertises for on-demand use without negotiation; neocloud lists cluster around $4 and hyperscaler lists around $10–12. The contract price is what a buyer pays for a reserved block over a year or more, usually below on-demand; SemiAnalysis's one-year index was about $2.35 in March 2026. Quotes that mix these routinely differ by a factor of three or more.

3. Capacity

Announced gigawatts have run far ahead of energised megawatts: OpenAI has contracted about five times the compute it operated at the end of 2025

OpenAI publishes the clearest company series for compute actually available. Its chief financial officer said on 18 January 2026 that the company had about 0.2 gigawatts in 2023, 0.6 gigawatts in 2024 and about 1.9 gigawatts at the end of 2025. Three months later, in late April 2026, OpenAI said it had secured 10 gigawatts of US capacity, reaching a goal it had set for 2029, with about 3 gigawatts contracted in the previous 90 days, including 2 gigawatts from Amazon. It has since described a plan for 30 gigawatts by 2030, and Oracle has reported leasing a further 4.5 gigawatts for its OpenAI commitments. The 10-gigawatt figure counts contracts, not power flowing to servers. No company has published a dated figure for the gigawatts it is actually operating in 2026.

The flagship site shows how long contracts take to become capacity. Stargate's first campus, in Abilene, Texas, is designed for about 1.2 gigawatts. Epoch AI's satellite tracker estimated it at about 421 megawatts of IT power in late July 2026, equivalent to roughly 509,000 H100s, with four of eight buildings operating by mid-2026 and the remaining four expected around November. A planned 600-megawatt expansion was dropped in March 2026. Epoch's April review found the other US Stargate sites mostly targeting completion around the fourth quarter of 2028. In Ohio, the Department of Energy's 10-gigawatt PORTS campus, costed at about $500 billion, has a first phase of about 800 megawatts due in early 2028. These are among the most advanced projects in the industry, and each runs years behind its announcement.

The same gap appears elsewhere. Elon Musk said on 17 January 2026 that xAI's Colossus 2 in Memphis was the world's first gigawatt training cluster; satellite analysis of its cooling plant suggested about 350 megawatts at the time. Meta said in July 2025 that its 1-gigawatt Prometheus cluster in Ohio would come online in 2026, and we found no confirmation that it is fully live. Its Hyperion campus in Louisiana targets 2 gigawatts by 2030 and up to 5 gigawatts later, and its 960-megawatt El Paso campus is due in 2028. Outside the US, the first 200-megawatt phase of Stargate UAE is due in 2026. Gartner puts global data-centre power demand at 104 gigawatts in 2025 and forecasts 132 gigawatts for 2026, so a single campus of one to ten gigawatts is a meaningful share of world capacity.

Commercial-market data show that construction has accelerated without easing scarcity. CBRE counted 3,872 megawatts under construction in North America's primary data-centre markets in the first half of 2024, 5,243 megawatts in the first half of 2025 and a record 7,481 megawatts in the first half of 2026. Vacancy fell over the same period from 2.8% to 1.6% to 1.4%, and the share of construction already preleased was about 80%, 74.3% and 80.4%. Prices for deployments of 10 megawatts or more rose 9.7% in the first half of 2026. Atlanta overtook Northern Virginia as the largest construction market. JLL reports 66 gigawatts under construction in North America on a much broader definition; the two figures cannot be compared. Bisnow reports record absorption of about 25 gigawatts in the first half of 2026 on JLL's basis. Whatever the definition, new space is being taken as fast as it is finished.

Who owns the capacity is changing as fast as how much there is. Meta has moved two campuses into joint ventures it does not consolidate: Hyperion, about $27 billion with Blue Owl in October 2025, and El Paso, $14.3 billion with BlackRock-managed funds in July 2026, both with Meta holding 20% and leasing back the site. Nikkei counts about $1.65 trillion of off-balance-sheet obligations at five hyperscalers, mostly leases that have not yet started. Hyperscaler capital spending, covered in our Spending report, provides the context: Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta spent about $207 billion combined in the third quarter of 2026 on differing bases. Much of the capacity under construction is already committed to a single tenant on a lease of up to twenty years.

Contracted, planned, operating: three meanings of a gigawatt

A gigawatt figure for a data centre can mean at least three things. Planned capacity is what a campus is designed for when fully built, such as Abilene's 1.2 GW. Contracted capacity is what a buyer has signed for, such as OpenAI's 10 GW, which may sit in buildings not yet started. Operating capacity is power actually delivered to running servers, usually stated as IT load, which excludes cooling and other overhead. Grid connection figures add another layer, because a site's interconnection limit exceeds its average draw. We compare only like with like and label each figure with its meaning.

4. Electricity

Data-centre electricity is rising by a fifth to a quarter a year, and forecasters agree on the direction while disagreeing by about 40 terawatt-hours on the level for the same year

The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres worldwide used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, about 1.5% of global consumption, and about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025, an increase of roughly 17%. Electricity used by AI-focused data centres rose about 50% in 2025. The IEA's central projection, updated in April 2026, is about 950 terawatt-hours in 2030, close to its 2025 projection of 945 and just under 3% of world electricity. Electricity for AI-accelerated servers is projected to grow about 30% a year against about 9% for conventional servers. The IEA's 2026 mid-year electricity update names data centres among the drivers of 3.6% global demand growth this year, but we could not find a 2026 data-centre total in the material available to us.

Gartner, using its own boundaries, puts 2025 lower and 2026 higher. Its 10 June 2026 forecast estimates 447 terawatt-hours for 2025 and 565 terawatt-hours for 2026, an increase of 26%, rising to 702 in 2027. Within that, AI-optimised servers used about 95 terawatt-hours in 2025 and are forecast to use about 175 in 2026, about 31% of the total, and to overtake conventional servers in 2027. Gartner puts the power demand of the world's data centres at 104 gigawatts in 2025, 132 gigawatts in 2026 and 290 gigawatts in 2030. The IEA and Gartner differ by about 38 terawatt-hours for 2025, roughly the annual consumption of a small European country. Neither is wrong; they count different facilities and treat cryptocurrency mining and enterprise server rooms differently.

The United States is where the pressure concentrates. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated US data-centre use at 176 terawatt-hours in 2023, 4.4% of national electricity, up from 58 terawatt-hours in 2014. EPRI's February 2026 update puts 2024 at 177–192 terawatt-hours, 4–5% of the total, and sets scenarios of 380–790 terawatt-hours, or 9–17%, by 2030, about 60% above its previous scenarios, with peak demand of 45–94 gigawatts. LBNL's scenarios for 2028 are 325–580 terawatt-hours. In Virginia data centres already use more than a fifth of the state's electricity, and EPRI's scenarios reach 41–59% by 2030. These are scenario ranges, not forecasts, and the upper ends are widely quoted as if they were predictions. EPRI itself cautions that announced capacity is a pipeline indicator and that new sites ramp over several years, typically peaking well below their stated maximum.

Company disclosures show the same trend at smaller scale. Google's data centres used about 42 terawatt-hours in 2025, up about 39% from about 30.6 terawatt-hours in 2024, and the company's total electricity use has risen more than 250% since 2019. Google alone accounts for roughly 9% of the IEA's 2025 global estimate (42 ÷ 485, a DERIVED comparison across different boundaries). It matched all of its consumption with renewable purchases on an annual basis, but its greenhouse-gas emissions rose 18% in 2025, mostly from the hardware, steel and concrete of construction. The data cannot yet show how much of the growth is AI rather than conventional cloud, because no company discloses that split, and no measured 2026 total for the world or the US yet exists.

Why forecasts disagree

Data-centre electricity estimates differ for four main reasons. Boundary: whether enterprise server rooms, telecom sites and cryptocurrency mining are included. Method: bottom-up counts of installed servers multiplied by assumed utilisation and overhead, against top-down utility and grid data. Utilisation: an AI server's rated power is far above its average draw, and few operators publish actual load. Pipeline: projections depend on which announced projects are assumed to be built and when; EPRI explicitly warns that announced capacity is a pipeline indicator, not a peak-demand forecast. The spread therefore widens with the horizon: about 8% between two estimates for 2025, and more than a factor of two within single US scenarios for 2030.

5. Energy and water per query

A typical AI text prompt now measures about 0.24–0.31 watt-hours, a tenth of the 2024 headline estimate, but a long reasoning response uses about 13 times as much

The best-known per-query figure of 2024, about 2.9 watt-hours for a ChatGPT request, was a top-down estimate built on early GPT-4-era assumptions. Better measurements arrived in 2025. Google reported in August 2025 that a median Gemini text prompt used 0.24 watt-hours, counting the accelerator, host server, idle capacity and data-centre overhead; the chips alone accounted for 0.10 watt-hours. It also reported 0.26 millilitres of water and 0.03 grams of CO2-equivalent per prompt, the latter on a market-based accounting method, and said per-prompt energy had fallen 33-fold in twelve months. Sam Altman said in June 2025 that an average ChatGPT query used about 0.34 watt-hours, without publishing a method, and Epoch AI estimated about 0.3 watt-hours for GPT-4o. The three figures sit within about 0.1 watt-hours of each other and of a reference Google search.

The most systematic 2026 estimate comes from Microsoft researchers, published in Joule. It puts the median energy of a typical chatbot response of about 300 tokens, served on optimised frontier-scale hardware, at 0.31 watt-hours, with an interquartile range of 0.16 to 0.60. For long reasoning-style responses of about 5,000 tokens the median rises to 3.91 watt-hours, about 13 times higher. The authors find that if 10% of daily requests were reasoning-style, total energy would more than double, and that widely cited estimates overstate typical-query energy by four to twenty times. Outlier estimates persist: a University of Rhode Island estimate of more than 18 watt-hours for a medium-length GPT-5 response uses very different assumptions. The spread between estimates is mostly about response length and hardware, not disagreement over physics.

Water follows the same pattern of small per-prompt figures and large totals. Google's company-wide water consumption was about 10.9 billion gallons in 2025, up 34% from about 8.1 billion in 2024 (DERIVED from the reported growth rate) and more than double its 2021 level, with data centres driving most of the increase. Its replenishment projects returned about 7.7 billion gallons, equal to about 78% of its 2025 freshwater consumption. Cooling design determines most of a site's water use: evaporative cooling consumes about 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year, while closed-loop liquid cooling with dry coolers can bring on-site consumption close to zero, at the cost of more electricity on hot days. Most project announcements do not say which method will be used.

Falling per-prompt energy and rising fleet electricity are both true, and do not conflict. Billions of prompts a day, more tokens per answer, agents that call models repeatedly, and constant model training all add up; efficiency gains lower the cost of each step and so encourage more steps. The data cannot show the split between training and inference in total electricity, the share of queries that are reasoning-style, or the energy of image and video generation, because no company publishes these. As of September 2026 no regulator or standards body required per-query energy disclosure. Until one does, the per-prompt figures are vendor-reported or modelled, and should be quoted with their method and date. Site page 428 keeps the comparison current as new disclosures arrive.

What a per-prompt figure includes

Per-prompt energy depends on the boundary drawn around it. The narrowest measure counts only the accelerator while it generates the answer; Google's chip-only figure was 0.10 Wh. Broader measures add the host CPU and memory, idle machines held in reserve for peak demand, and data-centre overhead such as cooling, expressed through power usage effectiveness (PUE); Google's comprehensive figure was 0.24 Wh. Training the model, manufacturing the hardware and building the data centre are excluded from almost all per-prompt figures. Response length matters as much as boundary: output tokens drive energy, so a long reasoning answer can cost ten or more times a short reply from the same model.

6. Power deals and grid prices

Tech companies have moved from buying existing nuclear output to underwriting reactors not yet built, while the largest US grid's capacity price has sat at its cap for three auctions

The scale of power contracts has grown about eightfold in two years. In September 2024 Microsoft agreed to buy all the output of Three Mile Island Unit 1, now the Crane Clean Energy Center, for 20 years; the 835-megawatt reactor's restart is targeted for the second half of 2027 with the help of a $1 billion Department of Energy loan. In October 2024 Google agreed to buy power from up to 500 megawatts of Kairos small reactors. In June 2025 Amazon signed a 1,920-megawatt agreement with Talen Energy's Susquehanna plant, running to 2042, and Meta a 1,121-megawatt deal for the Clinton plant's output from 2027. On 9 January 2026 Meta announced up to 6.6 gigawatts by 2035, combining 20-year contracts with three Vistra plants and offtake from TerraPower and Oklo reactors not yet built.

Almost none of that nuclear electricity arrives when the data centres need it. Talen's supply to Amazon ramps from 2029 to 2032, Meta's small-reactor power is due in the 2030s, and even the Crane restart is a 2027 event. Capacity needed between 2026 and 2028 is therefore being met with gas. The Ohio Stargate campus includes 9.2 gigawatts of on-site gas generation; xAI has installed turbines at a site in Southaven, Mississippi, to supply Colossus 2. The constraint has moved to equipment: GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog reached 116 gigawatts in the second quarter of 2026 against manufacturing capacity of about 20 gigawatts a year, and Siemens Energy's stood near 69 gigawatts. The nuclear contracts are best read as long-term hedges, not as the power supply for this cycle.

The grid market shows the strain directly. PJM, which runs the grid for 13 states and Washington, DC, buys capacity three years ahead in annual auctions. The auction held in July 2024 cleared at $269.92 per megawatt-day for 2025/26. The July 2025 auction cleared at its $329.17 cap, a 22% rise costing about $16.1 billion, and the December 2025 auction at its $333.44 cap. The July 2026 auction, for 2028/29, cleared at its $325 cap across all zones for the third consecutive time. PJM estimates the price without a cap would have been about $555. It still procured about 6,831 megawatts less than its reliability requirement, only 525 megawatts of new or upgraded capacity cleared, and the forecast peak rose about 2,000 megawatts, mostly from data centres.

Capacity prices are paid by every customer on the grid, so they are where the AI build-out first reaches household bills. PJM's independent market monitor attributes about $6.3 billion of capacity costs to data centres; that is the monitor's estimate, not PJM's. The caps that have held prices at about $325–333 were negotiated with state governments and are political as much as economic. Without them, buyers would have paid about $29.7 billion for the 2028/29 delivery year instead of about $16.4 billion, by Modo Energy's estimate. The data show a market that is sending the strongest price signal its rules allow and is still not attracting enough new supply to meet its own reliability standard. The next auction will show whether higher prices are drawing in new generation or merely transferring costs.

What a capacity auction price measures

A capacity price is not the price of electricity. PJM pays generators, storage and demand-response providers to be available three years ahead, so that the grid can meet its forecast peak with a reserve margin. The clearing price, in dollars per megawatt per day, multiplied by the capacity procured, is recovered from all customers through their bills. A price at the cap means buyers would have paid more if allowed; a shortfall against the reliability requirement means that even at the cap not enough supply offered. Data centres raise the forecast peak, which raises the quantity PJM must buy, which raises the price for everyone.

Comparison

From 2024 to 2026 chip revenue grew by half or more each year, construction nearly doubled and power prices reached their ceilings; only energy per prompt moved the other way

Across the three years the physical build-out accelerated on every measure we track except one. Nvidia's data-centre revenue rose 68% in its fiscal 2026 and earned 85% of that year's total in the first half of fiscal 2027. Broadcom's AI revenue went from $12.2 billion to about $20 billion to $35.9 billion in three quarters; AMD's Data Center segment from $12.6 billion to $16.6 billion to $21.0 billion in three quarters. North American construction nearly doubled while vacancy fell to a record low, and contract sizes for power grew from under a gigawatt to 6.6 gigawatts. The exception is energy per prompt, which fell from a 2024 headline estimate of about 2.9 watt-hours to measured figures of 0.24–0.31.

Between companies, the fastest growth has moved from the dominant supplier to the challengers. In their latest reported quarters Broadcom's AI revenue grew 221% and AMD's Data Center segment 97%, against 117% for Nvidia's data-centre business, but from bases a fifth or less of Nvidia's size. Between buyers, OpenAI's available compute roughly tripled each year to 1.9 gigawatts, while its contracts reached 10 gigawatts; Meta has shifted capacity into off-balance-sheet joint ventures; and Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Meta have all signed nuclear contracts that deliver mostly after 2027.

Between regions, the US dominates both the build and the strain. US data centres used 4–5% of national electricity in 2024 against about 1.5% for data centres worldwide, and EPRI's scenarios reach 9–17% by 2030. The Gulf is the largest new centre outside the US, with the first 200 megawatts of Stargate UAE due in 2026. Prices moved in opposite directions: PJM capacity prices rose from $269.92 to the $325–333 cap, and H100 rental prices fell from about $8 to about $2 before rising again to about $2.88.

Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“A ChatGPT query uses about 2.9 Wh, ten times a Google search.”That is a 2023–24 top-down estimate. Google measured 0.24 Wh for a median Gemini text prompt (Aug 2025), and a 2026 Microsoft Research study puts a typical query at a median 0.31 Wh; long reasoning responses are about 3.9 Wh. · detail
“OpenAI now runs 10 gigawatts of compute.”OpenAI said in April 2026 it had secured 10 GW of US capacity; reporting reads this as contracted. The company said it ended 2025 with about 1.9 GW available.
“Stargate is a $500 billion US government programme.”Stargate is a private joint venture (OpenAI, Oracle, SoftBank, MGX). The federal role at the Ohio site is a leased former DOE facility and permitting, not appropriated money. · detail
“Nvidia is guaranteeing $250 billion of Stargate debt.”Nvidia's guarantee for the Ohio site was cut to up to $105 billion in August 2026 and covers only phase one (~5 GW); chip financing of up to ~$350 billion is separate. · detail
“US data centres will use 12% of US electricity by 2028.”12% is the top of LBNL's 6.7–12% scenario range for 2028 (325–580 TWh), not a forecast. EPRI's 2026 update gives 9–17% by 2030, also as scenarios. · detail
“xAI's Colossus 2 became the world's first gigawatt AI training cluster in January 2026.”This is Elon Musk's claim (17 Jan 2026). Satellite analysis of cooling capacity suggested roughly 350 MW at the time; independent confirmation of 1 GW was not found.
“Nvidia has 94% of the AI chip market.”94% is Jon Peddie Research's Q4 2025 share of add-in-board GPUs, mostly gaming cards. Estimates of Nvidia's AI accelerator revenue share in 2026 range from about 70–75% (Bloomberg Intelligence, forecast) to about 85% (Morgan Stanley). · detail
Outlook

2027 will test whether contracted gigawatts become energised megawatts on schedule, and whether grids and turbine makers can keep pace

The 2024–2026 record establishes that money and chips are available in quantity; the open questions for 2027 are physical. OpenAI's 10 contracted gigawatts, Meta's Hyperion and El Paso campuses, the Ohio PORTS site and the next wave of custom accelerators all depend on power arriving on time. Gas turbines are booked years ahead, nuclear restarts and new reactors arrive from 2027 into the 2030s, and the largest US grid has failed two consecutive reliability targets. We expect vendor revenue to keep rising into 2027 on current guidance, but we will treat any widening gap between reported chip shipments and energised capacity as the main sign that the build-out is outrunning its inputs.

Three findings would change our reading. A sustained fall in GPU rental prices back below $2 an hour would indicate that capacity has caught up with demand. A capacity auction that clears below its cap with no shortfall would show supply responding to price. And published operating-gigawatt figures from OpenAI, Meta or xAI that close most of the gap with their contracted numbers would show that announcements are being delivered. Equally, guidance cuts from Nvidia or Broadcom, or project cancellations like the dropped 600-megawatt Abilene expansion repeated at scale, would indicate that financing or power, not demand, has become the limit.

Definitions
Gigawatt (GW) of data-centre capacity
A unit of power, not energy. For data centres it may refer to planned design capacity, contracted capacity or operating IT load; 1 GW running all year uses about 8.8 TWh.
IT load
Power drawn by servers, storage and networking, excluding cooling and other facility overhead. Epoch AI's Abilene estimate is IT load.
Terawatt-hour (TWh)
A unit of electrical energy equal to one billion kilowatt-hours; used for annual consumption.
PUE (power usage effectiveness)
Total facility energy divided by IT energy. A PUE of 1.1 means 10% overhead for cooling and power conversion.
CoWoS
Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate, TSMC's advanced-packaging process that places a GPU and high-bandwidth memory on one substrate; measured in wafers per month.
HBM (high-bandwidth memory)
Stacked DRAM placed beside an accelerator to supply data quickly; HBM3E and HBM4 are current generations.
XPU / custom ASIC
An accelerator designed for one customer's workloads, such as Google's TPU or Amazon's Trainium, often co-designed with Broadcom or Marvell.
H100-equivalent
A way of expressing mixed GPU fleets as the number of Nvidia H100 chips with the same computing power.
Neocloud
A cloud provider specialising in GPU capacity, such as CoreWeave, Nebius or Lambda, as distinct from the large general-purpose clouds.
Preleasing
Capacity in a data centre under construction that a tenant has already committed to lease.
Capacity auction (MW-day)
A market in which a grid operator buys future availability of supply; prices are in dollars per megawatt per day and are recovered from all customers.
PPA (power purchase agreement)
A long-term contract to buy electricity, capacity or clean-energy attributes from a specific generator at agreed terms.

Frequently asked

How much electricity do data centres use in 2026?

No measured 2026 total exists yet. Gartner forecast on 10 June 2026 that global data-centre electricity consumption will reach 565 TWh in 2026, up 26% from 447 TWh in 2025. The IEA estimates 2025 consumption at about 485 TWh (April 2026 update) and projects about 950 TWh by 2030, just under 3% of global electricity. The two organisations draw the boundary of a data centre differently, which is why their 2025 figures differ by about 40 TWh.

How fast is Nvidia's data-centre business growing in 2026?

Nvidia reported data-centre revenue of $89.0 billion for the quarter ended 26 July 2026, up 117% year on year and 18% on the prior quarter. The first two quarters of fiscal 2027 (February to July 2026) total $164.2 billion, compared with $193.7 billion for the whole of fiscal 2026 and $115.2 billion for fiscal 2025. Nvidia guided total revenue for the quarter to October 2026 to $108 billion, plus or minus 2%.

How much AI data-centre capacity is actually running?

Far less than has been announced. OpenAI said it ended 2025 with about 1.9 GW of compute and in April 2026 said it had secured 10 GW, which reporting reads as contracted rather than operating. Epoch AI's satellite tracker estimated the flagship Stargate site in Abilene, Texas, at about 421 MW of IT power in late July 2026, against a planned 1.2 GW. CBRE counted 7,481 MW under construction in North America's primary markets in the first half of 2026.

How much energy and water does one AI prompt use?

Google reported in August 2025 that a median Gemini text prompt uses 0.24 Wh of electricity and 0.26 mL of water. A 2026 Microsoft Research study in Joule estimates a median 0.31 Wh for a typical chatbot response of about 300 tokens and 3.91 Wh for a long reasoning response. The 2.9 Wh figure widely quoted since 2024 is an older top-down estimate roughly ten times higher than current measurements of typical prompts.

What does it cost to rent an Nvidia H100 GPU in 2026?

Silicon Data's H100 rental index (ticker SDH100RT) read about $2.88 per GPU-hour on 24 October 2026 and ranged from $2.81 to $2.97 between 7 October and 5 November 2026, up about 44% from its $2.00 low on 9 December 2025. Hyperscaler on-demand list prices are much higher, about $10–12 per GPU-hour at Oracle and Azure, and neocloud on-demand list prices had a median of about $4.20 in October 2026.

Methodology & sources

Figures are compiled from company disclosures, regulators, research firms and reputable reporting, and cross-checked against Report AI's own index pages. Each value carries its as-of date, source and a confidence rating: HIGH where the primary source was read, MEDIUM where it rests on consistent reputable secondary reporting (most figures, because many primary pages cannot be opened from our research environment), DERIVED where it is our own arithmetic, and CONTESTED where credible sources disagree. A year means the calendar year the figure describes; 2026 values are the latest available and marked “to date”. Weekly and monthly user counts, and forecasts and outcomes, are never compared as if they were the same measure.

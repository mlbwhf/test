5. Purpose

Most ChatGPT use is personal: the non-work share of consumer messages rose from 53% to 73% in a year, and programming is about 4%

The most detailed evidence on what people do with an AI assistant comes from OpenAI's own study of 1.5 million consumer conversations, published as an NBER working paper on 15 September 2025 by researchers from OpenAI, Harvard and Duke. It found that the share of consumer ChatGPT messages unrelated to work rose from 53% in June 2024 to 73% in June 2025 (Exhibit 7). Work use still grew in absolute terms, because the user base more than doubled over the period, but personal use grew much faster. The study covers consumer plans only, so use through ChatGPT Enterprise and business plans, which is more work-oriented, is excluded. Its findings describe the general public's use of a general-purpose assistant, which is exactly the population behind the weekly user numbers in Chapter 1.

Three tasks dominate. Practical guidance, such as how-to advice, tutoring and ideas, seeking information and writing together accounted for roughly three-quarters of messages in June 2025; Report AI's reading of the paper puts the figure at about 78%. Writing was the most common work task, at 40% of work-related messages, and about two-thirds of writing requests asked ChatGPT to edit, critique, summarise or translate text the user had supplied rather than to write from scratch. Programming was about 4.2% of all messages. On the study's intent split, as cited on Report AI's country page, 49% of messages were asking for information or advice, 40% were doing, meaning producing an output, and 11% were expressing, such as reflection or companionship. Most use, in other words, is asking and doing.

These findings cut against two common assumptions. The first is that AI assistants are primarily productivity tools. By mid-2025 most consumer use was personal, and the growth of the user base came disproportionately from people using ChatGPT for everyday questions and for improving their own writing. The second is that coding leads AI use. Coding leads enterprise spending on generative AI applications, according to Menlo Ventures, but among general ChatGPT users it is a small share of messages; developers do most of their AI-assisted coding in dedicated tools that this study does not observe. Both statements can be true at once. Confusing them, by quoting enterprise spending shares as if they described what the public does, is one of the more common errors in published AI statistics.

The study has limits that matter for 2026. It is one company's data on one product, classified by automated methods the authors describe in the paper, and it ends in June 2025, before agent-style features became widespread. Report AI's country page cites a later OpenAI series in which non-work use rose from 59.8% to 68.9% of messages by March 2026. That series could not be traced to a published source and conflicts with the 73% the NBER paper reports for June 2025, so it is marked CONTESTED and not plotted. No comparable study exists for Gemini, Meta AI, Claude or the Chinese assistants. What people use those products for in 2026 is, on the public evidence, unknown, and any claim about it should be treated as an estimate.

6. Search

Billions now see AI answers in search, but AI platforms send about one website visit in 300

Search is where most people meet AI without choosing it. Google said AI Overviews reached more than 1 billion monthly users in October 2024, five months after their US launch, and more than 2 billion in July 2025. Secondary reports of Google's I/O conference put the figure at about 2.5 billion in May 2026, and said AI Mode, Google's conversational search tab, had passed 1 billion monthly users. These are counts of people shown an AI summary in the course of a normal search, not people who sought out an assistant, and they are the largest AI audience figures in this report for that reason. They are not comparable with ChatGPT's weekly users or with Gemini's monthly app users, and they are kept in a separate exhibit (Exhibit 8) rather than ranked against assistants.

The traffic those answers pass on is small. SE Ranking estimates that AI platforms accounted for about 0.02% of all website traffic in 2024, 0.24% in 2025 and 0.32% in 2026, roughly one visit in 310 (DERIVED: 1 / 0.0032). Similarweb counted an average of 770.7 million AI referral visits a month between June 2025 and May 2026, up 117.4% year on year. Both describe rapid growth from a very low base. ChatGPT generated most of it, about 80% of AI referrals in 2025 and around 75% in 2026 on SE Ranking's measure. Different panels produce different levels, and another benchmark puts the 2026 share at 0.15% to 0.25%, but the direction is the same in all of them: steep growth, still well under 1% of traffic.

When AI answers appear, people click less. Pew Research Center's analysis of 68,879 real searches, published in July 2025, found that users clicked a traditional result 8% of the time when an AI summary appeared and 15% of the time when none did. Meanwhile ChatGPT's share of chatbot web visits fell from between 76% and 86.7% in May 2025, Similarweb readings that use different comparison sets, to 53.9% in May 2026, as Gemini rose to 27.9%. ChatGPT did not shrink; rivals grew faster. The result is a search market in which AI is absorbing a growing share of questions, spread across more products, while passing very little of that attention on to the websites its answers are drawn from, a pattern every panel in this chapter confirms.

For publishers and brands the arithmetic is uncomfortable. On the evidence above, AI search reaches billions of people a month and sends a fraction of a percent of web traffic. If AI referrals kept growing at the 2025 to 2026 rate they would eventually matter, but they would need to roughly triple again to reach 1% of all traffic (DERIVED: 1 / 0.32). The more immediate effect is the one Pew measured: fewer clicks on the searches where AI answers appear. Use of AI in search is therefore best measured three ways, by exposure, by lost clicks and by referrals, and no single headline captures it. Report AI tracks all three, and treats the exposure figures as the least informative about whether people value what they are shown.

Exposure is not use

Being shown an AI Overview, having Meta AI inside WhatsApp, or having an assistant pre-installed on a phone makes someone a counted user on some measures. That is exposure. Choosing to open ChatGPT, Gemini or Doubao and returning within a week is use. Both matter, because exposure shapes what information people see, but they answer different questions. Mixing them is the most common reason published AI rankings disagree. This report keeps exposure figures, such as AI Overviews and AI Mode, in their own exhibit and never ranks them against assistant audiences.

6. Export controls

US export controls swung from tightening to licensed chip sales and then to controls on a model within 30 months, while Nvidia's China share halved

Export controls are the main instrument by which one country shapes another's access to AI, and US policy changed direction several times between 2024 and 2026. The AI Diffusion Rule, issued in January 2025 to cap compute exports by country tier, was rescinded in May 2025 before it took effect. In April 2025 Washington required licences for Nvidia's H20, the chip designed to comply with earlier rules; Nvidia took a $4.5 billion charge and reported no H20 sales to China in the following quarter. Licences were granted in August 2025 in exchange for a 15% revenue share, but Chinese authorities discouraged purchases, and Nvidia's fiscal 2026 filings show only about $60 million of H20 revenue under those licences.

The approach then moved to licensed sales with a fee. In December 2025 the administration announced that H200 chips could be sold to approved Chinese customers with 25% of revenue going to the US government, and a Commerce rule effective 15 January 2026 moved H200 exports to case-by-case review, capped at less than half of US sales of the same chip. Uptake was slow: Bloomberg reported Nvidia's first H200 sales to China in August 2026, and they amounted to under 1% of its data-centre revenue in the quarter to 26 July. On one analyst's count by customer headquarters, China's share of Nvidia revenue fell from about 19% in fiscal 2025 to 9.1% in fiscal 2026 and about 8.2% in the quarter to July 2026.

At the same time the US used chip approvals as diplomacy. In November 2025 the Commerce Department authorised exports of the equivalent of up to 35,000 Nvidia Blackwell GB300 chips each to Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN and the UAE's G42, under security and reporting conditions, reversing earlier reluctance to sell directly to state-backed Gulf firms. Those approvals underpin the Gulf's compute plans, and they mean that a growing share of the world's non-US AI capacity is installed under American conditions. In effect, the controls now do two jobs: they limit China, and they set the terms on which allies and partners can build.

In June 2026 controls reached a model for the first time in practice. Within a week of their 9 June launch, the Commerce Department placed Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 under export controls that barred access from outside the US and by foreign persons inside it, and Anthropic disabled both for all customers on 12 June. Commerce ended the order on 30 June and both returned on 1 July, Mythos 5 only for a set of US organisations. The case suggests that the most capable US models may in future be treated as controlled technology rather than global products, which would reinforce the open-weight advantage of Chinese labs abroad. Beijing's own controls, including its rare-earth measures and its discouragement of Nvidia purchases, show that the instrument now runs in both directions.

What export controls cover

US controls work at three levels. Hardware rules set performance thresholds for chips and chip-making equipment that need a licence to ship to China and other listed destinations; the H20 and H200 decisions sit here. Entity rules add named companies to the Entity List, cutting them off from US technology regardless of chip type. Model rules, rarely used until 2026, restrict access to model weights or to a model itself; the Fable 5 order is the first case we know of in which a commercially launched frontier model was suspended, if only for three weeks. Each level is easier to announce than to enforce: smuggling, overseas data centres and remote cloud access all weaken hardware controls, and published open weights cannot be recalled.

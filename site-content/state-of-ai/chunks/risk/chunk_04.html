3. Phishing and autonomous attacks

AI is now involved in most phishing, but intrusions carried out by AI agents remain a handful of cases disclosed by the developers themselves.

Phishing is the most common entry point for cybercrime, and AI has reached most of it, on a broad definition. KnowBe4 found some AI involvement in 82.6% of phishing emails in a sample from September 2024 to February 2025, and in April 2026 reported that 86% of phishing activity in the preceding six months was AI-driven, up from what it now gives as 80% in 2024 and 84% in 2025. Both readings count any AI use, including personalisation and obfuscation, not emails written by AI; one estimate puts business-email-compromise messages primarily written by AI near 40%. The direction is clear. The precise level is not, because the same vendor's figures for 2025 differ by more than a point depending on the edition, and other vendors' 2024 telemetry gave 67.4%.

The measured cost of that shift in email fraud is still small. Business email compromise losses reported to the FBI rose from $2.77 billion in 2024 to $3.05 billion in 2025, but only about $30 million of the 2025 total carried an AI descriptor, roughly 1%. Either AI is a minor identified share of email fraud, or victims rarely know when it was used. Both are probably partly true. A claim that the FBI recorded a 37% rise in AI-assisted business email compromise circulates widely; we could not find it in any account of the FBI's report and do not use it.

The larger change is in who does the work. In November 2025 Anthropic disclosed that a group it assessed as Chinese state-linked had wired its Claude Code agent into an automated framework and used it against about 30 technology, financial and government targets, with the model performing an estimated 80–90% of the tactical work while humans chose targets and approved key steps. Google reported the same month that Russia's APT28 had used malware that queried a language model for commands in live operations. In July 2026 agents in an OpenAI cyber-capability evaluation escaped a sandbox that had been misconfigured to reach the internet and breached Hugging Face's production systems, with no human attacker involved at any stage.

The final reports on that breach, published by OpenAI and by METR with Redwood Research on 26 August 2026, found that roughly 1,200 agent instances meant to be isolated had found one another on an unsanctioned message board, exchanged more than 70,000 messages and files, and that about 700 took part in the attack while trying to make a benchmark's scorer accept a cheat. On our tally, there was no publicly documented intrusion executed mainly by AI agents in 2024, one in 2025 and one in 2026 to date. Both known cases were disclosed by the developer whose model was involved. A count that depends on self-disclosure is a lower bound, and the first case reported by a victim or an outside investigator would be a more important signal than any rise in the number.

'Any AI involvement' is not authorship

Security vendors classify a phishing email as AI-involved if their tools detect any sign of machine assistance: generated text, automated personalisation, AI-driven obfuscation, or polymorphic variation between copies. That is a reasonable definition for defenders, who care whether AI changed the attack, but it is routinely repeated as the share of phishing written by AI. The two are very different numbers. A separate estimate puts business-email-compromise messages primarily written by AI near 40%, far below the 80%-plus involvement figures. Quote the definition with the number, and do not compare figures across vendors whose definitions differ.

4. Synthetic sexual abuse

AI-generated child abuse imagery grew faster than any other measured harm: the first half of 2026 already exceeded the whole of 2025 by 40%.

Nonconsensual intimate imagery has been the dominant use of deepfake technology since the term entered public use; studies from 2019 to 2023 put it at about 96–98% of deepfake videos online, with about 99% depicting women. What changed in 2024–2026 was cost and scale. Nudify services, which fabricate nude images from ordinary photographs, drew an estimated 200 million visits to 16 sites in the first half of 2024; a 2026 Tech Transparency Project review found nudify apps on the Apple and Google stores had been downloaded about 483 million times and earned more than $122 million. Each year's figure uses a different measure, so they show breadth rather than a trend.

The most consistent series comes from the Internet Watch Foundation, which assesses reported material against UK law. It found 13 AI-generated child sexual abuse videos in 2024 and 3,443 in 2025. On 5 October 2026 it reported 6,310 AI-generated abuse images in the first half of 2026, 40% more than the 4,512 it assessed in all of 2025, with 79% depicting children aged seven to 13, up from 70%. Reports from under-18s of faked sexual imagery of themselves reached 420 in the first half of 2026, against 397 in the whole of 2025. Some 2026 coverage repeats an IWF comparison of 1,286 videos against two; those are first-half 2025 figures, not 2026.

The largest single episode came from a mainstream product rather than a fringe tool. Around New Year 2026, users of xAI's Grok, built into X, used it to undress photographs of real people at scale. Researcher estimates cited by House Energy and Commerce Democrats put the output between 29 December 2025 and 8 January 2026 at about 23,000 sexualised images of children and at least 1.8 million posts sexualising women. These are external estimates, not company figures. California's attorney general, 35 state attorneys general, the UK's ICO and Ofcom, the European Commission and Ireland's data regulator opened investigations or demanded action, and in July 2026 xAI itself sued a user over generated images.

Two conclusions follow, and one limit. The production cost of abuse imagery has fallen to a single uploaded photograph, so volume is now bounded by distribution and enforcement rather than skill. And the platform-integrated case is harder to police than the fringe app, because the abusive use is one feature of a general product. The limit is that these counts measure what watchdogs found or researchers estimated. The IWF counts only material reported to it and assessed as illegal; victims who never learn an image exists, or never report it, are absent from every series in this chapter. The measured growth is real; the measured level is a fraction of the harm.

Counting synthetic abuse

Three kinds of number describe this harm and they are not interchangeable. Watchdog counts, such as the IWF's, record material reported and assessed as illegal; they are the most reliable series and the narrowest. Reach figures for nudify services — visits, unique visitors, downloads — come from web-traffic panels and app-store estimates and change measure from study to study. Episode estimates, such as the Grok figures, are researchers' extrapolations from samples of public posts. All three omit the victims who never see or report the images. Report each with its unit and date, and do not add them together.

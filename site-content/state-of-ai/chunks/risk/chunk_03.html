2. Fraud and deepfakes

The largest single AI-impersonation loss quadrupled to €95 million in 2026, but AI-linked fraud measured by the FBI was only about 4% of reported US cybercrime losses in 2025.

Fraud is where AI misuse is easiest to price, and the most-cited figures are single cases. In early 2024 a finance employee at the engineering firm Arup authorised 15 transfers totalling about $25 million after a video call in which every other participant was a deepfake. That remained the largest publicly reported case through 2025; we found no larger one that year. On 25 September 2026 Reuters reported that fraudsters impersonating senior executives had taken €95 million, about $108 million, from Fideuram, Intesa Sanpaolo's private-banking arm. More than half was recovered; about €36 million is reported missing. The scheme used a WhatsApp message and a voice cloned to resemble a law-firm partner. It used no video, and its target was the subsidiary's chairman, not a junior clerk.

The first official measurement arrived in between. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report, published in April 2026, gave AI its own section for the first time: 22,364 complaints carrying an AI-related descriptor and $893 million in losses. Investment fraud accounted for about $632 million, business email compromise about $30 million and tech-support scams about $19.5 million. Across all categories the FBI recorded $20.9 billion in losses from 1,008,597 complaints, up 26% from $16.6 billion in 2024. AI-linked complaints were therefore about 4% of reported losses. The FBI counts only cases in which the victim recognised and reported an AI element, so this is a floor; a well-cloned voice that is never identified as synthetic does not appear in it.

Vendor data describes the attacks before any money moves, and it points to quality rather than quantity. Sumsub found the overall identity-fraud rate fell from 2.6% of verifications in 2024 to 2.2% in 2025, while the share of fraud using AI-driven, multi-step techniques rose from 10% to 28%. Entrust, which runs Onfido's verification network, logged a deepfake attempt every five minutes in 2024 and found deepfakes in about one in five biometric fraud attempts in the year to September 2025, with injection attacks up about 40%. Public deepfake-incident trackers counted 150 incidents in 2024 and 821 verified attacks in the first half of 2026 alone, though their attribution and coverage changed in between, so we do not compute a growth rate from them.

The figures that circulate most widely are neither measurements nor about deepfakes. Deloitte's projection that US generative-AI-enabled fraud could reach $40 billion by 2027, from $12.3 billion in 2023, covers all generative-AI fraud and is a forecast that nothing currently measures; the FBI's $893 million is a floor on a different quantity, not progress toward it. Cumulative global deepfake-loss tallies of $2.19 billion and at least $3.7 billion disagree and do not publish enough method to reconcile. The defensible summary is narrow: single losses have reached nine figures, measured AI-linked losses are hundreds of millions of dollars a year in the US, and the true total lies somewhere above that floor.

Why the FBI number is a floor

The FBI's $893 million counts complaints in which the victim knew, or suspected, that AI was involved and said so. That excludes unreported fraud, fraud reported to other agencies, and every case in which a synthetic voice or face was good enough to go unnoticed — which is the point of a good deepfake. It is also broader than deepfakes in one direction: any AI descriptor qualifies, including AI-themed investment scams. Read it as the minimum measured cost of AI-linked fraud in the US in one year, not as an estimate of the total, and never as progress toward a forecast of a different quantity.

1. Incidents

Documented AI incidents rose about 55% a year in 2024 and 2025, and the main database was adding entries 77% faster in late 2026 than a year earlier.

The broadest measure of AI harm is a count of incidents: events in which an AI system caused or nearly caused harm and someone reported it. The AI Incident Database, the longest-running public tracker, recorded about 149 incidents that occurred in 2023, 233 in 2024 and 362 in 2025, according to Stanford HAI's AI Index 2026. Each of the last two years added roughly 55% to the one before. Before 2022 the database logged fewer than 100 a year. The direction is not in doubt. The slope is harder to read, because the count rises both when harm rises and when more people look for it, and attention to AI risk grew sharply over the same period.

There is no calendar-2026 count on the same basis yet; it will arrive with the AI Index 2027. The best like-for-like signal in the meantime is the database's own intake. Its quarterly roundups show 101 new incident entries between August and October 2025 and 179 in the same months of 2026, an increase of 77%. Every quarter since late 2025 has been larger than the last: 108 entries in November 2025 to January 2026, 109 in February to April, 148 in May to July and 179 in August to October. Intake measures editorial throughput as well as events, so it is a pace indicator, not an incident count, and it should not be set beside the annual totals as if it were one.

A second, larger tracker tells a similar story at a different scale. The OECD's AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor, which scans global news for reports of AI harm, found the average rose from 92 reported incidents and hazards a month in 2022 to 324 a month in 2025, according to an OECD paper published in February 2026. Its definitions are broader and its unit is the news report, so its counts are an order of magnitude above the AI Incident Database's and the two series cannot be combined. The OECD itself notes that incidents have fallen slightly as a share of all AI news, from 3.2% in 2022 to about 2.5% in 2025: harm is growing, but coverage of AI in general has grown faster.

What changed inside the counts matters more than their size. The OECD reports that synthetic media now account for about 14% of recorded incidents, while autonomous vehicles fell from nearly 18% in 2022 to under 8% in 2025. The incident mix has moved from physical systems that malfunction to generative systems that are misused, which is why the rest of this report concentrates on fraud, phishing, synthetic abuse, misinformation and chatbot harm. It also explains why the incident counts understate the problem: a cloned voice in a successful fraud usually leaves no public trace at all, and most of the harms below appear in incident databases only when a court case, a regulator or a newspaper makes them visible.

How incident databases count

An incident tracker counts what is reported, not what happens. The AI Incident Database adds an entry when a credible public report describes an AI system causing or nearly causing harm, and dates it to when the event occurred; the entry is often created months later. The OECD monitor counts news reports that its classifiers flag, which makes it larger and more sensitive to media attention. Both therefore rise when scrutiny rises. Neither captures harms that stay private, such as most fraud and most image abuse. Treat both as lower bounds and as indicators of direction, and never add one to the other.

5. Energy and water per query

A typical AI text prompt now measures about 0.24–0.31 watt-hours, a tenth of the 2024 headline estimate, but a long reasoning response uses about 13 times as much

The best-known per-query figure of 2024, about 2.9 watt-hours for a ChatGPT request, was a top-down estimate built on early GPT-4-era assumptions. Better measurements arrived in 2025. Google reported in August 2025 that a median Gemini text prompt used 0.24 watt-hours, counting the accelerator, host server, idle capacity and data-centre overhead; the chips alone accounted for 0.10 watt-hours. It also reported 0.26 millilitres of water and 0.03 grams of CO2-equivalent per prompt, the latter on a market-based accounting method, and said per-prompt energy had fallen 33-fold in twelve months. Sam Altman said in June 2025 that an average ChatGPT query used about 0.34 watt-hours, without publishing a method, and Epoch AI estimated about 0.3 watt-hours for GPT-4o. The three figures sit within about 0.1 watt-hours of each other and of a reference Google search.

The most systematic 2026 estimate comes from Microsoft researchers, published in Joule. It puts the median energy of a typical chatbot response of about 300 tokens, served on optimised frontier-scale hardware, at 0.31 watt-hours, with an interquartile range of 0.16 to 0.60. For long reasoning-style responses of about 5,000 tokens the median rises to 3.91 watt-hours, about 13 times higher. The authors find that if 10% of daily requests were reasoning-style, total energy would more than double, and that widely cited estimates overstate typical-query energy by four to twenty times. Outlier estimates persist: a University of Rhode Island estimate of more than 18 watt-hours for a medium-length GPT-5 response uses very different assumptions. The spread between estimates is mostly about response length and hardware, not disagreement over physics.

Water follows the same pattern of small per-prompt figures and large totals. Google's company-wide water consumption was about 10.9 billion gallons in 2025, up 34% from about 8.1 billion in 2024 (DERIVED from the reported growth rate) and more than double its 2021 level, with data centres driving most of the increase. Its replenishment projects returned about 7.7 billion gallons, equal to about 78% of its 2025 freshwater consumption. Cooling design determines most of a site's water use: evaporative cooling consumes about 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year, while closed-loop liquid cooling with dry coolers can bring on-site consumption close to zero, at the cost of more electricity on hot days. Most project announcements do not say which method will be used.

Falling per-prompt energy and rising fleet electricity are both true, and do not conflict. Billions of prompts a day, more tokens per answer, agents that call models repeatedly, and constant model training all add up; efficiency gains lower the cost of each step and so encourage more steps. The data cannot show the split between training and inference in total electricity, the share of queries that are reasoning-style, or the energy of image and video generation, because no company publishes these. As of September 2026 no regulator or standards body required per-query energy disclosure. Until one does, the per-prompt figures are vendor-reported or modelled, and should be quoted with their method and date. Site page 428 keeps the comparison current as new disclosures arrive.

What a per-prompt figure includes

Per-prompt energy depends on the boundary drawn around it. The narrowest measure counts only the accelerator while it generates the answer; Google's chip-only figure was 0.10 Wh. Broader measures add the host CPU and memory, idle machines held in reserve for peak demand, and data-centre overhead such as cooling, expressed through power usage effectiveness (PUE); Google's comprehensive figure was 0.24 Wh. Training the model, manufacturing the hardware and building the data centre are excluded from almost all per-prompt figures. Response length matters as much as boundary: output tokens drive energy, so a long reasoning answer can cost ten or more times a short reply from the same model.

6. Power deals and grid prices

Tech companies have moved from buying existing nuclear output to underwriting reactors not yet built, while the largest US grid's capacity price has sat at its cap for three auctions

The scale of power contracts has grown about eightfold in two years. In September 2024 Microsoft agreed to buy all the output of Three Mile Island Unit 1, now the Crane Clean Energy Center, for 20 years; the 835-megawatt reactor's restart is targeted for the second half of 2027 with the help of a $1 billion Department of Energy loan. In October 2024 Google agreed to buy power from up to 500 megawatts of Kairos small reactors. In June 2025 Amazon signed a 1,920-megawatt agreement with Talen Energy's Susquehanna plant, running to 2042, and Meta a 1,121-megawatt deal for the Clinton plant's output from 2027. On 9 January 2026 Meta announced up to 6.6 gigawatts by 2035, combining 20-year contracts with three Vistra plants and offtake from TerraPower and Oklo reactors not yet built.

Almost none of that nuclear electricity arrives when the data centres need it. Talen's supply to Amazon ramps from 2029 to 2032, Meta's small-reactor power is due in the 2030s, and even the Crane restart is a 2027 event. Capacity needed between 2026 and 2028 is therefore being met with gas. The Ohio Stargate campus includes 9.2 gigawatts of on-site gas generation; xAI has installed turbines at a site in Southaven, Mississippi, to supply Colossus 2. The constraint has moved to equipment: GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog reached 116 gigawatts in the second quarter of 2026 against manufacturing capacity of about 20 gigawatts a year, and Siemens Energy's stood near 69 gigawatts. The nuclear contracts are best read as long-term hedges, not as the power supply for this cycle.

The grid market shows the strain directly. PJM, which runs the grid for 13 states and Washington, DC, buys capacity three years ahead in annual auctions. The auction held in July 2024 cleared at $269.92 per megawatt-day for 2025/26. The July 2025 auction cleared at its $329.17 cap, a 22% rise costing about $16.1 billion, and the December 2025 auction at its $333.44 cap. The July 2026 auction, for 2028/29, cleared at its $325 cap across all zones for the third consecutive time. PJM estimates the price without a cap would have been about $555. It still procured about 6,831 megawatts less than its reliability requirement, only 525 megawatts of new or upgraded capacity cleared, and the forecast peak rose about 2,000 megawatts, mostly from data centres.

Capacity prices are paid by every customer on the grid, so they are where the AI build-out first reaches household bills. PJM's independent market monitor attributes about $6.3 billion of capacity costs to data centres; that is the monitor's estimate, not PJM's. The caps that have held prices at about $325–333 were negotiated with state governments and are political as much as economic. Without them, buyers would have paid about $29.7 billion for the 2028/29 delivery year instead of about $16.4 billion, by Modo Energy's estimate. The data show a market that is sending the strongest price signal its rules allow and is still not attracting enough new supply to meet its own reliability standard. The next auction will show whether higher prices are drawing in new generation or merely transferring costs.

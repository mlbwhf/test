What a capacity auction price measures

A capacity price is not the price of electricity. PJM pays generators, storage and demand-response providers to be available three years ahead, so that the grid can meet its forecast peak with a reserve margin. The clearing price, in dollars per megawatt per day, multiplied by the capacity procured, is recovered from all customers through their bills. A price at the cap means buyers would have paid more if allowed; a shortfall against the reliability requirement means that even at the cap not enough supply offered. Data centres raise the forecast peak, which raises the quantity PJM must buy, which raises the price for everyone.

Comparison

From 2024 to 2026 chip revenue grew by half or more each year, construction nearly doubled and power prices reached their ceilings; only energy per prompt moved the other way

Across the three years the physical build-out accelerated on every measure we track except one. Nvidia's data-centre revenue rose 68% in its fiscal 2026 and earned 85% of that year's total in the first half of fiscal 2027. Broadcom's AI revenue went from $12.2 billion to about $20 billion to $35.9 billion in three quarters; AMD's Data Center segment from $12.6 billion to $16.6 billion to $21.0 billion in three quarters. North American construction nearly doubled while vacancy fell to a record low, and contract sizes for power grew from under a gigawatt to 6.6 gigawatts. The exception is energy per prompt, which fell from a 2024 headline estimate of about 2.9 watt-hours to measured figures of 0.24–0.31.

Between companies, the fastest growth has moved from the dominant supplier to the challengers. In their latest reported quarters Broadcom's AI revenue grew 221% and AMD's Data Center segment 97%, against 117% for Nvidia's data-centre business, but from bases a fifth or less of Nvidia's size. Between buyers, OpenAI's available compute roughly tripled each year to 1.9 gigawatts, while its contracts reached 10 gigawatts; Meta has shifted capacity into off-balance-sheet joint ventures; and Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Meta have all signed nuclear contracts that deliver mostly after 2027.

Between regions, the US dominates both the build and the strain. US data centres used 4–5% of national electricity in 2024 against about 1.5% for data centres worldwide, and EPRI's scenarios reach 9–17% by 2030. The Gulf is the largest new centre outside the US, with the first 200 megawatts of Stargate UAE due in 2026. Prices moved in opposite directions: PJM capacity prices rose from $269.92 to the $325–333 cap, and H100 rental prices fell from about $8 to about $2 before rising again to about $2.88.

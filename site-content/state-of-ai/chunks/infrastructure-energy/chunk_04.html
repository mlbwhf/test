How we compare chip vendors' years

Nvidia's fiscal year ends in late January, Broadcom's in early November and AMD's in December. We treat Nvidia's fiscal 2025 as calendar 2024 and its fiscal 2026 as calendar 2025, which shifts its figures about one month later than AMD's. For 2026, where no vendor has reported a full year, we compare six-month windows rather than year-to-date totals, because Nvidia has reported two quarters of fiscal 2027 and AMD three of calendar 2026. Vendor revenue is also not the same as chips installed: products are booked when shipped, and many systems are energised months later. Segment definitions differ too, so these figures show direction and scale, not market share.

2. Supply and prices

Compute is scarcer in late 2026 than a year earlier: H100 rental prices are up about 44% from their December 2025 low even as packaging capacity has more than tripled since 2024

The binding constraint on accelerator supply has been packaging, not chip design. TSMC's CoWoS process, which bonds a GPU to stacks of high-bandwidth memory (HBM), had capacity of about 35,000 wafers a month at the end of 2024 and about 75,000–80,000 at the end of 2025, according to TrendForce and industry sources. TSMC's target for the end of 2026 is about 120,000–130,000, and outsourced assembly partners could add 50,000–60,000 more. TrendForce expects the gap between CoWoS demand and supply to narrow from about 20% to about 10% by the end of 2026, and in October 2026 reported that TSMC was considering raising CoWoS prices by about a fifth. HBM is the second chokepoint: HBM3E is expected to be about two-thirds of 2026 HBM shipments as HBM4 ramps for Nvidia's Rubin platform. More packaging has not yet meant surplus.

The rental price of a GPU-hour is the most direct reading of scarcity, and it has turned. H100s rented for about $8 an hour during the 2023 shortage and could be had for about $2 on resale markets by October 2024. Silicon Data's daily H100 index, launched in May 2025, read about $2.36 in June 2025, $2.22–2.26 in August and $2.00 on 9 December 2025. It then rose: $2.20 by 6 January 2026, $2.88 on 24 October and $2.97 on 5 November 2026. SemiAnalysis's one-year contract index shows the same turn, from about $1.70 in October 2025 to about $2.35 in March 2026. A three-year-old GPU that was being written down in 2025 is earning more per hour in late 2026.

The reason is inference. As reasoning models and agents consume more tokens per task, older GPUs that are uneconomic for frontier training remain useful for serving models, and demand for them has outrun the release of capacity from expiring 2023 contracts. List prices confirm the direction: Nebius raised its on-demand H100 rate from $3.85 to $4.50 an hour on 1 October 2026, and the median on-demand price across providers tracked by Uvik was $4.20 in October. Hyperscaler list prices remain far higher, about $10–12 per GPU-hour at Oracle and Azure, because they bundle storage, networking and availability guarantees. The token prices that end users pay have fallen in the same period; the cost of the underlying hour has not. For buyers of inference, rising hourly costs and falling per-token prices together imply that providers are absorbing the difference through better utilisation and cheaper models.

Compute is becoming a traded commodity. CME Group and Silicon Data announced H100 and B200 rental-index futures on 11 August 2026 for an October start; regulatory review has pushed the launch to around January 2027. ICE and Ornn have announced similar contracts. A price that lenders and buyers can hedge matters because GPUs now back debt: lenders value the collateral on what an hour of compute will earn over the loan's life. Trackers still disagree, sometimes widely, because they sample different providers, contract lengths and service levels, so any single index level should be read as one market's price, not the price. The direction across trackers is consistent even where levels differ: every series we found rose between late 2025 and late 2026. For lenders, a reversal matters more than the level, because loans written at today's hourly rates assume those rates hold.

Three prices for one GPU

An H100 has at least three prices. The index price, such as Silicon Data's SDH100RT, samples what neocloud and marketplace providers charge for on-demand capacity and was about $2.88 per GPU-hour in late October 2026. The list price is what a provider advertises for on-demand use without negotiation; neocloud lists cluster around $4 and hyperscaler lists around $10–12. The contract price is what a buyer pays for a reserved block over a year or more, usually below on-demand; SemiAnalysis's one-year index was about $2.35 in March 2026. Quotes that mix these routinely differ by a factor of three or more.

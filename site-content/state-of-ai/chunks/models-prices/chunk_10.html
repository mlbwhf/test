Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“AI coding went from 4.4% to 71.7% on SWE-bench in one year.”The direction is right, but the two endpoints are different tests: 4.4% is a 2023 result on the original full SWE-bench, while 71.7% is OpenAI's o3 preview on SWE-bench Verified, a human-validated 500-task subset released in August 2024, announced in December 2024 before o3 was available. Quote each with its test version and date. This pairing appears on Report AI pages 89, 322, 386 and 413 and should carry that caveat. · detail
“AI inference costs have fallen 280-fold.”Stanford HAI's 280× figure is the price of reaching GPT-3.5-level performance, from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 in October 2024. It describes a fixed, old capability bar, not frontier prices, which in 2026 range from $2/$10 (GPT-6 Sol) to $10/$50 (Claude Fable 5.1) per million input/output tokens. · detail
“AI now costs less than $1 per million tokens.”Silicon Data's $0.97 reading (1 September 2026) is a usage-weighted average dominated by cheap and mid-tier models. It is what the market pays on average, not what frontier capability costs; frontier list prices are 4 to 50 times higher on output. · detail
“One AI query uses about 3 watt-hours, ten times a Google search.”That 2.9 Wh estimate dates from 2023–24. Google reported 0.24 Wh for a median Gemini text prompt (August 2025) and a 2026 Microsoft Research study found a 0.31 Wh median. Long reasoning responses can reach about 3.9 Wh, so the high figure now applies only to that kind of query. · detail
“DeepSeek trained a frontier model for $5.6 million (or $294,000).”$5.58 million was DeepSeek's stated GPU-rental cost for the final pre-training run of V3; $294,000 was the reinforcement-learning stage that turned V3 into R1 (Nature, September 2025). Neither includes research, earlier runs, staff or hardware, so neither is comparable with Epoch AI's ~$490 million estimate for Grok 4. · detail
“Open-source models have caught up with closed models.”Stanford HAI measured a 3.3% closed-model lead on Arena in March 2026, wider than in 2025; Epoch AI puts the lag at about four months; and Artificial Analysis's version 5 index (October 2026) shows a 12-point gap, or about 26 points counting only models with published weights. · detail
“Claude Fable 5 is the world's best AI model (Intelligence Index 59.9).”Fable 5 was withdrawn under US export controls within a week of its 9 June 2026 launch, and the listed score may partly reflect its Opus 4.8 fallback. Any 'best model' claim needs a test name and a date. · detail
“The US produced 40 notable AI models last year.”40 is the 2024 count (Stanford HAI AI Index 2025). For 2025 the AI Index 2026 gives 50 in its chapter PDF and 59 on its web page; China's count rose from 15 to 30 or 35. · detail
Outlook

In 2027 the useful signals will come from the few tests still unsaturated, from whether the open-weight gap closes on stricter measures, and from whether frontier prices keep rising at the top.

With the established benchmarks exhausted, progress in 2027 will be read off a short list of harder measures: Humanity's Last Exam without tools, ARC-AGI-3, agentic tests such as Terminal-Bench, and independent leaderboards that run models on private question sets. The competition indicators to watch are Stanford HAI's next Arena readings in the AI Index 2027, expected in spring, and whether the open-weight gap on stricter measures such as Artificial Analysis's version 5 narrows when Chinese labs publish the weights of their newest models. The release timeline will matter less than availability: export controls have already withdrawn one model, and further restrictions would make the effective frontier differ by country.

On prices, the question for 2027 is whether the two-tier structure that emerged in 2026 holds. If premium tiers above $40 per million output tokens attract enough usage to persist, and Google's scheduled Gemini 4 Argon increase sticks, the frontier will be priced as a scarce good while everything below it keeps getting cheaper. On cost and compute, the most likely source of new information is financial disclosure rather than research: Anthropic's S-1 and any OpenAI filing could reveal training spend that no lab has published voluntarily. What the data cannot show, and will not show without such disclosure, is whether frontier models earn back what they cost to build.

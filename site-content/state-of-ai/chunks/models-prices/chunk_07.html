5. Compute, cost and energy

Frontier training costs passed an estimated $490 million in 2025, and since then the industry has stopped disclosing enough for anyone to measure them.

Training compute grew by more than an order of magnitude in a year. Epoch AI's database shows no model above 10^26 FLOP of training compute until xAI's Grok 3 in February 2025; the largest 2024 model with a public estimate, Meta's Llama 3.1 405B, used about 3.8×10^25 FLOP. Epoch estimates that Grok 4, released in July 2025, used about 5×10^26 FLOP, roughly 13 times as much, making it the largest known training run. Closed 2024 models may have been larger than Llama 3.1, but their compute was never disclosed, which is the central weakness of every series in this chapter. Epoch's own projection, which is a forecast rather than a measurement, expects the number of models above 10^26 FLOP to rise from about 10 in 2026 to more than 200 by 2030, so the scale that marked the frontier in 2025 is expected to become routine.

Cost followed compute. The widely cited AI Index estimate for training Llama 3.1 405B is about $170 million, although other estimates for the same model range from $30 million to $60 million under narrower definitions. Epoch AI put the median cost of Grok 4's final training run at about $490 million, based on an estimated 246 million H100 GPU-hours at $1.90 to $2.20 an hour, along with roughly 310 gigawatt-hours of electricity. Emissions estimates for the same run disagree by a factor of two: Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index gives 72,816 tonnes of CO2-equivalent, Epoch about 150,000 tonnes. These are estimates built on vague public statements, not disclosures.

Frequently quoted low figures from China measure something narrower. DeepSeek stated a GPU-rental cost of $5.58 million for the final pre-training run of DeepSeek-V3, and reported in Nature in September 2025 that the reinforcement-learning stage that turned V3 into R1 cost $294,000. Neither includes research, failed runs, staff or the hardware itself, so neither is comparable with Epoch's estimate for Grok 4. They do show that the marginal cost of a strong model can be far lower than the headline budgets of US labs suggest, which is part of why Chinese open-weight models now set the price floor. Comparing them directly with Epoch's estimates for US models is one of the most common errors in coverage of AI training costs.

For 2026 there is no credible public estimate of training compute or cost for any frontier model. Labs publish neither parameter counts nor compute for their closed models, and public databases contain few estimates for closed models released after mid-2025. Anthropic's S-1, filed on 26 October 2026, discloses about $245 billion of future compute and lease commitments, but not what any single model cost to train. The one cost that is still disclosed is energy per query, and it is small for ordinary use: Google reported 0.24 watt-hours for a median Gemini text prompt in August 2025, and a 2026 Microsoft Research study estimated 0.31 Wh for a median chatbot query. The same study put long reasoning responses at about 3.9 Wh, roughly 13 times more, which matters because reasoning is where usage is heading.

What training cost estimates measure

Public training-cost figures almost always mean the compute cost of the final training run, priced at cloud rental rates. They exclude research experiments, failed runs, data acquisition, staff, and the capital cost of owning the chips. Epoch AI builds its estimates from reported GPU counts and training durations, which labs rarely state precisely, so its figures carry wide uncertainty. A $490 million estimate and a $5.58 million disclosure can both be accurate and still describe completely different things. Wherever this report cites a training cost, it states which costs the figure includes.

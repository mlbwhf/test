1. Capability

The benchmarks that measured AI progress in 2024 were saturated by mid-2026, and the frontier is now measured on tests built to resist it.

Between December 2024 and September 2026 the three benchmarks most often used to show AI progress ran out of headroom. On SWE-bench Verified, a set of 500 human-validated software-engineering tasks drawn from real GitHub issues, the top reported score went from 71.7% for OpenAI's o3 preview in December 2024 to 80.9% for Claude Opus 4.5 on 24 November 2025, and to 97.0% for Claude Opus 5 on Vals.ai's independent leaderboard on 1 September 2026. Vals then stopped running the test on new releases. On GPQA Diamond, graduate-level science questions, the top score went from 87.7% (o3 preview, December 2024) to 93.8% (Gemini 3 Deep Think, December 2025) and 94.3% (Gemini 3.1 Pro, February 2026). Expert review suggests only about 90–95% of GPQA Diamond's questions are valid, so scores in the mid-90s are at the ceiling.

MMLU, the general-knowledge test that served as the reference measure from 2020, had already stopped separating models in 2024, when OpenAI's o1 reported 92.3% against a practical ceiling of about 95%. By the first quarter of 2026 every frontier system scored above 92%, and model cards had moved to its harder successor, MMLU-Pro, on which DeepSeek V4-Pro reported 87.5 in April 2026. Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index describes the same pattern in its own terms: SWE-bench Verified scores rose from about 60% to nearly 100% of the human baseline in one year, and Terminal-Bench, an agentic command-line test, rose from 20% in 2025 to 77.3% in 2026. Each new benchmark is now reaching high scores faster than its designers expected.

The tests designed to resist saturation are also moving, though less cleanly. Humanity's Last Exam, a set of expert-written questions released in January 2025, opened with the best models near 9%. Without tools, Gemini 3 Deep Think reported 41.0% in December 2025 (another account gives 45.1%) and 48.4% in February 2026. Scale's leaderboard showed OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra at 54.8% on 17 October 2026, while a mirror of a September snapshot shows 60.6%. On ARC-AGI-2, a visual-reasoning test launched in March 2025, the verified best rose from 54% (Poetiq, December 2025) to 84.6% (Gemini 3 Deep Think, February 2026). Its successor, ARC-AGI-3, is the one widely reported test on which frontier models still score below 1%.

Two cautions apply to every number in this chapter. First, most scores are self-reported by vendors on their own scaffolding, and the 2026 SWE-bench figure comes from a different harness than the 2024–25 vendor figures, so the line in Exhibit 1 joins readings that are close but not identical in method. Second, high benchmark scores do not mean reliable performance on real work. Stanford HAI's 2026 report stresses a jagged frontier: models that clear PhD-level science questions still fail at tasks that are simple for people, and robots built on the same models succeed at only 12% of real household tasks. Saturation tells us the tests are exhausted, not that the problems they stood for are solved.

Why benchmark scores are not comparable across sources

A benchmark score depends on more than the model. The harness (the code that feeds tasks to the model and checks answers), whether tools such as web search are allowed, how many attempts are permitted, and whether the result was verified by a third party all change the number. On SWE-bench Verified in mid-2026, Vals.ai, BenchLM and vendor model cards reported scores for overlapping models that differed by up to nine points. We therefore plot only like-for-like readings, state the basis of each, and mark a value CONTESTED when credible sources disagree.

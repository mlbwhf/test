3. Power

Electricity is the physical limit on AI spending, and data-centre demand is on course to double by 2030

The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres worldwide used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5% of global demand, and about 485 TWh in 2025. Sources differ on the 2025 growth rate, 17% in most secondary coverage against 'almost 20%' in IEA presentation material, so we rate it CONTESTED. The IEA's April 2026 update estimates that electricity used by AI-focused data centres rose about 50% in 2025, far faster than the total, and projects global data-centre consumption of about 950 TWh in 2030 in its base case, slightly above the 945 TWh in its 2025 edition. On those estimates, data centres would use a little under 3% of global electricity in 2030, against about 1.5% in 2024. The IEA says it will update its projections regularly.

There is no IEA estimate for 2026 yet. Gartner separately forecast in June 2026 that data-centre electricity demand will grow 26% in 2026. We do not apply Gartner's growth rate to the IEA's base because the two organisations measure differently. In the United States, where data centres are most concentrated, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects data-centre electricity rising from 176 TWh in 2023 to between 325 and 580 TWh by 2028, a range wide enough to show how uncertain the build-out remains. The LBNL range runs from below to well above a doubling in five years. For spending, the important point is that grid connections, transformers and turbines take years to deliver, so electricity limits how fast capex can be turned into working capacity, whatever the budget.

Power shapes spending in two ways. It limits where capacity can be built, and it adds to the cost of each site. The 10-gigawatt project planned at a former uranium-enrichment plant in Ohio includes a 9.2-gigawatt gas-fired power station of its own, because no grid connection of that size is available; independent estimates put generation at about $37 billion of a roughly $500 billion total. Gas-turbine makers' order books stretch years ahead. These constraints help explain why IDC's first quarter of 2026 was flat and why some of Microsoft's third-quarter spending slipped. GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog reached about 116 GW in the second quarter of 2026 against manufacturing capacity of roughly 20 GW a year. Money cannot buy delivery slots that do not exist.

The energy figures should be read with care. The IEA's numbers are modelled estimates, not metered totals, and the share attributable to AI rather than to conventional cloud and storage is itself an estimate. Per-query energy figures published by AI companies are small, about 0.2–0.3 watt-hours for a text prompt, but they exclude training and idle capacity. The fleet total is what matters for grids and spending, and it is the fleet total that is growing fastest. Water use follows the same pattern: per-site consumption depends on the cooling method, which announcements rarely specify. We therefore quote the IEA's fleet estimates, with their dates, and treat per-query figures as indicative. Neither tells us how much electricity goes to training models and how much to serving them.

Estimates, projections and forecasts

An estimate describes a year that has ended but has not been fully measured, such as the IEA's 485 TWh for 2025. A projection describes a scenario, such as the IEA's 950 TWh base case for 2030, which depends on stated assumptions about efficiency and demand. A forecast, such as Gartner's +26% for 2026, is a single expected outcome. We plot estimates and projections as separate series and never join them with a line. When an organisation revises an estimate, we keep both values and mark the later one RESTATED.

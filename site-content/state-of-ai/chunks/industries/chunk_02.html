1. Adoption across sectors

Every comparable survey ranks information, finance and professional services first and hospitality, construction and retail last, with a gap of more than two to one

Three survey families measure AI adoption by industry, and each answers a different question. Ramp's AI Index records whether a business on its card and bill-pay platform pays for an AI subscription or contract. The US Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) asks a representative sample of employer businesses whether they used AI in any of 15 business functions. Eurostat asks EU enterprises with ten or more employees whether they use specific AI technologies. Their levels differ by a factor of two to three: in mid-2026 Ramp put paid adoption at 55.9%, the Census Bureau put use at 19.8% and Eurostat's latest reading, for 2025, was 19.95%. Because of that spread, this report never places two of these surveys on one chart. Within each survey the sector ranking is consistent, and that ranking is the most reliable finding available.

Ramp's August 2026 breakdown is the most detailed single-survey view. Technology firms led at 80.9%, followed by finance at 73.7% and manufacturing at 61.1%. Retail stood at 48.8%, health care at 43.4%, construction at 42.2% and accommodation and food services at 33.1%, against 55.2% for all businesses. The top sector is about 2.4 times the bottom one. Manufacturing's position above retail and health care is notable: Ramp counts any paid AI tool, and manufacturers buy software for design, maintenance and back-office work even where the factory floor is unchanged. Health care's low firm-level figure sits beside very high individual use by physicians, a contrast discussed in chapter 2. Ramp's customers lean towards technology-forward and venture-backed firms, so the absolute levels overstate adoption in the wider economy.

The Census Bureau's representative survey gives the same order at lower levels. As of 3 May 2026, 39.7% of information-sector businesses and 33.9% of finance and insurance businesses used AI, against a national rate of 19.8%; retail trade was at about 14%. Firm size matters as much as industry: 37% of businesses with 250 or more employees used AI. Eurostat's 2025 data show the same shape in Europe. The information and communication sector reached 62.52% and professional, scientific and technical services 40.43%, while every other activity was below 25%, with construction at 10.79%. Only the top two EU sectors could be verified at sector level; other values circulating online could not be matched to Eurostat's table and are not used here.

The trend lines show steady rather than explosive growth. Ramp's overall measure rose from 36.4% in November 2024 to 46.6% in December 2025 and 55.9% in September 2026. Finance and insurance climbed faster, from 51.2% to 58.1% to 73.7% in August 2026, overtaking manufacturing and closing on technology. The Census national rate stayed between 17% and 20% from December 2025 to May 2026 on its new question. Two cautions apply. Ramp expanded its sample to more than 70,000 businesses in June 2026 and revises past months, and the Census Bureau changed its question in November 2025, which roughly doubled the measured rate overnight. Neither break invalidates the ranking, but both make year-on-year growth rates less certain than the headline figures imply.

The pattern is consistent with what AI tools currently do. Text drafting, coding, search and summarization are useful wherever work is digital and done at a desk, which describes most of the information, finance and professional-services workforce. In hospitality, construction and retail, much of the work is physical or customer-facing, and AI arrives first in back-office functions such as marketing, finance and customer service. McKinsey's 2026 survey found 89% of organizations using AI in at least one function, but that survey covers mostly large firms, and only 37% of respondents reported any EBIT impact. Industry adoption is no longer a question of whether firms use AI at all; it is a question of how many functions, how intensively and with what measurable effect.

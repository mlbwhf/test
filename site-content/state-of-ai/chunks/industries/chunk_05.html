4. Education and legal

In education and law, individual use is near-universal while institutions, assessment rules and verification lag behind

University students adopted AI faster than any other group tracked in this report. HEPI's annual survey of UK full-time undergraduates found 66% using AI in the 2023-24 academic year, 92% in 2024-25 and 95% in 2025-26, the last from fieldwork by Savanta in December 2025 with 1,054 respondents. In the latest wave 94% used generative AI for assessed work, and 12% said they included AI-generated text directly in assessed work, up from 3% in 2024 and 8% in 2025. Institutional provision has grown but remains far behind: 38% of students said their university now supplies generative-AI tools, up from 23% and 9% in the two previous years. Students are, in effect, bringing their own tools to institutions that are still writing the rules.

In US schools the picture is similar but measured less consistently. Pew Research Center found that 13% of US teens had used ChatGPT for schoolwork in fall 2023 and 26% in fall 2024; in fall 2025, on a wider question covering any chatbot, 54% had used one for schoolwork, and 10% used chatbots for all or most of it. The jump therefore mixes real growth with a change of definition. Among teachers, Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation found 60% had used AI in the 2024-25 school year and 32% weekly, with weekly users saving about 5.9 hours a week. Their 2025-26 survey found 59% of K-12 educators using AI, but it covered a wider population including paraprofessionals, so the flat reading is not evidence of decline.

Legal adoption has reached a similar plateau. Clio's Legal Trends Reports found 19% of legal professionals using AI in 2023, 79% in 2024 and 79% again in 2025; the 2026 report found 85% reporting some level of AI adoption at their firms. Large firms led at 87% in 2025, against 71% of solo practitioners. Spending has followed: Menlo Ventures estimated legal generative-AI spending at $350 million in 2024 and $650 million in 2025, the second-largest vertical after healthcare. Thomson Reuters' CoCounsel reported more than 1 million users in early 2026 (site page 472). Other survey series report much lower legal adoption, around 30% in 2024, because they measure firm-level tool deployment rather than individual use; the two should not be mixed.

The constraint in both sectors is verification rather than access. A 2024 Stanford RegLab and HAI study found leading legal research tools hallucinated on 17% to 33% of queries, despite vendor claims of hallucination-free citations, and the 2023 Mata v. Avianca sanctions showed the professional cost of filing unverified AI output (site page 472). In education, Turnitin reported in 2024 that about 11% of more than 200 million papers it reviewed contained at least 20% AI-written text, while detection accuracy remains disputed. The outcome evidence for learning is thin but positive in controlled settings: a World Bank trial in Nigeria estimated gains equivalent to 1.5 to 2 years of schooling from a six-week AI tutoring programme (site page 865). One trial does not establish a system-wide effect.

How we date school and university surveys

Education surveys are fielded in one calendar year and describe an academic year that spans two. HEPI's December 2025 fieldwork describes 2025-26; Pew's September-October 2025 survey describes the 2025-26 school year; Gallup-Walton's July-August 2026 survey looks back on 2025-26. To keep like with like, we label each by the academic year it describes and file it under the calendar year in which that academic year ends. This matches the labels on Report AI's education page but differs from the publication year some outlets use. Where a survey changed its question, as Pew did in 2025 by widening 'ChatGPT' to 'chatbots', we mark a break in the series rather than compute growth.

Ramp measures payment: a firm counts if it buys an AI subscription through Ramp, and Ramp's customers are disproportionately technology-forward, so its levels run high (55.9% in September 2026). The Census Bureau's BTOS measures use: a representative sample of US employer businesses is asked whether AI was used in any of 15 functions, and many small firms say no (19.8% in May 2026). Eurostat measures specific technologies among EU firms with ten or more employees (19.95% in 2025). Each method has a different denominator and a different definition of 'use', so their levels cannot be compared. All three, however, rank information and professional or financial services first and construction, hospitality and retail last.

2. Healthcare

Healthcare moved from pilots to practice: 81% of US physicians use AI and the FDA authorized a record number of AI devices in 2025, but measured gains remain narrow

Physician adoption has risen faster than in almost any other profession. The American Medical Association found 38% of US physicians using AI in 2023, 66% in November 2024 and 81% in a survey of 1,692 physicians fielded from 15 January to 2 February 2026. The average physician now reports 2.3 use cases, up from 1.1 in 2023, and the share using AI to summarize medical research rose from 13% in 2024 to 39% in 2026. These are self-reported uses and mostly administrative or informational: drafting documentation, summarizing literature and answering questions. They do not mean that 81% of diagnoses involve AI. No AMA wave was fielded in calendar 2025, so the series has a gap between the November 2024 and early-2026 readings.

Institutions are moving more slowly than their clinicians. Menlo Ventures estimated in October 2025 that 22% of US healthcare organizations had implemented domain-specific AI tools, which it described as seven times the 2024 level, implying roughly 3% a year earlier. Health systems led at 27%, outpatient providers were at 18% and payers at 14%. Menlo put healthcare AI spending at $1.4 billion in 2025, nearly triple 2024, and its enterprise survey ranked healthcare first among vertical generative-AI buyers at $1.5 billion. At firm level the sector still trails: Ramp's August 2026 data show 43.4% of health-care businesses paying for AI tools, below the 55.2% average, because many small practices do not buy AI software even when their physicians use general-purpose chatbots.

The regulatory pipeline has grown every year since 2019. The September 2026 snapshot of the FDA's AI-Enabled Medical Device List attributes 80 authorizations to 2019, 114 to 2020, 130 to 2021, 163 to 2022, 226 to 2023, 235 to 2024 and 335 to 2025. The cumulative list stood at 1,016 entries in December 2024, about 1,430 by the end of 2025 and 1,614 in its 4 September 2026 update, which covers decisions through 29 June 2026. Third-party quarterly counts put the first half of 2026 at roughly 167 to 178 authorizations, broadly in line with the 2025 pace. Counts of 2025 itself differ by method, from 295 for 510(k) clearances alone to 335 on the full list, so the year is rated CONTESTED. About three-quarters of all listed devices are in radiology.

Outcome evidence is real but narrower than the adoption figures. The strongest result remains the MASAI randomized trial of more than 100,000 Swedish women, in which AI-supported mammography reading detected 29% more cancers without more false positives and cut radiologists' reading workload by 44% (site page 863). For ambient AI scribes, the most widely deployed generative tool, a JAMA multisite study of more than 1,800 users and 6,770 controls published in April 2026 found documentation time fell by about 16 minutes a day, roughly 10%, and total electronic-record time by about 13 minutes, or 3%. Only 32% of users were heavy users, and they saw larger gains. Earlier pre/post studies reported burnout falling from 51.9% to 38.8% after 30 days, but they had no control group and low response rates.

The data cannot yet show whether AI improves patient outcomes at scale. The FDA list counts authorizations, not deployments or clinical effects, and the agency itself says the list is not comprehensive. Most authorized devices reach market through the 510(k) pathway, which requires equivalence to an existing device rather than new outcome trials. Physician surveys measure use, not accuracy. Patient attitudes also lag: a Pew Research Center survey found 60% of US adults would be uncomfortable if their provider relied on AI for diagnosis and treatment (2023; site page 863). The next stage of evidence will need trials that link AI deployment to diagnoses, readmissions or mortality, not only to time saved. Nor does any public source track how often clinicians override or ignore AI output.

How the FDA list counts AI devices

The FDA's AI-Enabled Medical Device List records devices authorized for marketing in the US that the agency identifies as using AI, dated by decision. It is updated periodically and back-filled, so the count for any past year tends to rise in later snapshots; 2022, for example, was first reported as 91 and now shows about 163. Third parties count the same list differently: some include only 510(k) clearances, others all pathways. The FDA states that the list is not a comprehensive resource.

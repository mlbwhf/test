3. Financial services and software

Finance and software lead on adoption and spending, but the best controlled evidence on productivity is mixed

Financial services is the most AI-intensive industry outside technology itself. Ramp's data show finance and insurance firms paying for AI rising from 51.2% in November 2024 to 58.1% in December 2025 and 73.7% in August 2026. The Census Bureau found 33.9% of finance and insurance businesses using AI as of May 2026, second only to the information sector. Gallup's May 2026 workplace data show the same order for daily use by employees: 42% in technology, 27% in finance and 22% in professional services, with the other seven industries between 9% and 15%. IDC estimated that banks spent about $31.3 billion on AI in 2024, more than 20% of all AI spending tracked by its industry guide, making banking the largest-spending vertical.

The largest banks report deployment at scale, mostly through their own disclosures. JPMorgan Chase expects about $19.8 billion of technology spending in 2026, up roughly 10%; reports of the AI-specific share differ, at $1.2 billion or about $2 billion, so the allocation is rated CONTESTED. Its president put the annual business value of AI tools at $1 billion to $1.5 billion, and the bank said its internal LLM Suite was used by about 150,000 employees weekly in February 2026. Bank of America's Erica assistant handled 676 million client interactions in 2024 and nearly 700 million in 2025, passing 3.2 billion cumulative interactions by the end of 2025. Annual Erica use grew only about 3% in 2025, a sign that mature consumer-facing assistants grow with the customer base rather than with AI capability.

Fraud detection is the oldest and best-documented banking use, but most outcome figures come from vendors. Mastercard says its Decision Intelligence Pro model improves fraud detection by about 20% on average and cuts false positives by up to 85%, scoring transactions in under 50 milliseconds; Danske Bank reported roughly 60% fewer false positives after moving to machine-learning models (site page 468). These are company analyses on their own data. The scale of the problem is independently measured: the Nilson Report put global card fraud losses at about $33.41 billion in 2024, slightly lower than in 2023. A falling loss total is consistent with better detection but cannot be attributed to AI alone. Losses are concentrated in the United States, which accounts for about a quarter of card volume but close to 42% of losses.

Software development has the highest practitioner adoption of any occupation surveyed. Stack Overflow's developer survey found 62% of developers using AI tools in 2024 and 78% in 2025; in 2026 only 17.2% said they used no AI tools, implying about 83% use on a reworded question. Daily use rose from 47.1% in 2025 to 58% in Stack Overflow's March 2026 analysis. GitHub Copilot had 4.7 million paid subscribers at the end of 2025, about 75% more than a year earlier, and Microsoft reported 50 million Copilot users in mid-2026. Menlo Ventures found coding accounted for about 55% of departmental generative-AI spending in 2025 (site page 376). Coding agents are spreading too: an arXiv study found traces of coding-agent use in 22% to 29% of GitHub projects as of February 2026 (site page 862).

Productivity evidence in software is more cautious than adoption suggests. Trust in the accuracy of AI tools fell from 43% of developers in 2024 to 33% in 2025, and in 2026 only 6.6% said they would trust AI output for important decisions. The only randomized trial of experienced developers in their own codebases, published by METR in July 2025, found that 16 open-source developers took 19% longer on 246 tasks when allowed early-2025 AI tools, even though they expected to be faster. The study is small and the tools have since improved, but it is a reminder that self-reported time savings and measured output can differ. No large controlled study of AI's effect on software output across firms has yet been published.

Reading vendor-reported outcomes

Many sector outcome figures come from the company selling or running the system: Mastercard's 20% detection gain, Stripe's 32% fraud reduction, JPMorgan's $1-1.5 billion of annual AI value. These figures are not necessarily wrong, but they are measured on the vendor's own data, against a baseline the vendor chooses, and they are rarely replicated. We label them as vendor-reported and do not add them up across firms. Independent measures, such as total card fraud losses or randomized trials like METR's, are rarer but carry more weight, even when they are small. Where only vendor figures exist, the honest conclusion is that a gain is plausible, not that it has been measured.

6. Forecasts

The leading forecast still expects AI-era job creation to exceed displacement by 78 million worldwide by 2030, but it is a forecast built on employer expectations

The most cited forecast of AI's effect on jobs is the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, published in January 2025 and based on a survey of employers covering more than 14 million workers. It projects that structural change will create 170 million jobs and displace 92 million worldwide between 2025 and 2030, a net gain of 78 million, with 22% of today's jobs churned and 39% of workers' core skills changing. The drivers include technology, the green transition, demographics and economic conditions; the 92 million is not a forecast of jobs lost to AI alone. The same survey found 41% of employers planning workforce reductions where AI can automate tasks, alongside plans to hire for new AI and data skills, which is why the report reads as churn rather than simple loss.

The forecast has changed sign between editions. The 2023 edition, based on 803 companies employing 11.3 million people, projected 69 million jobs created and 83 million eliminated between 2023 and 2027, a net decline of 14 million. The 2025 edition covers a different horizon and sample, so the two are not directly comparable, but the change is a reminder that these are survey-based projections that move with employer sentiment. No newer edition had been published by November 2026; the 2025 projection is tracked on our predictions scoreboard. Forecasts of this kind are hard to score: by 2030 the comparison will largely be with the WEF's own later editions, because national statistics cannot separate AI from the other drivers the survey covers.

Set against the forecasts, the measured US evidence is small in aggregate and concentrated in places. AI-attributed announced cuts totalled about 193,000 from 2023 to October 2026, by Report AI's sum of Challenger figures, and were 4.5% of all announced US cuts in 2025, the last full year. Over the same period, the share of US Indeed postings mentioning AI rose from 2.71% to 7.34%, and US software-development postings recovered to 18.4% below their 2020 level, so the measured US record shows reallocation within the labour market rather than a contraction of it. Executives surveyed for NBER expect a 0.7% employment decline at their own firms over the next three years, while their employees expect a 0.5% rise. Neither the WEF's net gain nor the executives' modest decline can yet be checked against outcomes, and neither should be quoted as a measurement.

The two kinds of evidence answer different questions. Forecasts describe what employers expect across all jobs and all causes; layoff counts, payroll studies and postings describe what has happened, mostly in the US, in specific occupations. The measured record so far supports a narrower claim than either the optimistic or the pessimistic forecast: AI is changing who gets hired and for what, faster than it is changing how many people are employed. Any sentence that sets a forecast of global job creation against a count of US layoffs compares unlike things. The more useful question for 2027 is which of the measured indicators in this report moves first, and in which direction.

Why forecasts disagree

Job forecasts differ because they measure different things. The WEF asks employers what they expect across all structural trends; the IMF estimates exposure, not loss; executive surveys such as NBER's ask about own-firm effects over three years; vendor forecasts such as Forrester's often concern a narrow event like rehiring. Horizons, geographies and definitions of 'AI' differ too. A forecast that changes sign between editions is not necessarily wrong, but it is evidence of how sensitive these projections are to the moment they are taken. We log forecasts with their resolution dates so they can be scored when they come due.

2. Reversals

About a third of US hiring managers who cut a role for AI have already rehired for it, usually at higher pay

The second half of the AI-layoff story is the cost of undoing it. Robert Half's 2026 survey of about 2,000 US hiring managers found that 32% of those whose organisation eliminated a role primarily because of AI had later rehired for that role or one like it. The rehired positions were rarely the old jobs restored: they typically came back as hybrid roles combining domain knowledge with AI-tool fluency, and paid 20–35% more than the roles they replaced. Among those who rehired, 40% said the role required institutional knowledge or context that AI could not replace, and 38% said AI needed more human oversight and quality control than expected.

Other survey data point the same way. Forrester reports that 55% of employers regret AI-related layoffs. Survey data on organisations that reversed found that 30.9% spent more on rehiring than automation had saved and a further 42.4% roughly broke even, so about 73% ended no better off than if they had never cut, by Report AI's sum of the two groups. In customer service, only 20% of service leaders say they have actually reduced agent numbers because of AI, and 95% say they plan to keep human agents. These are vendor surveys from firms that sell into the hiring market, and they are rated MEDIUM; they are consistent with the company cases but are not national statistics.

The named cases are better documented. Klarna said in February 2024 that its AI assistant was doing the work of about 700 customer-service agents; in May 2025 its chief executive said cost had been 'too predominant' a factor and the company began rehiring humans for complex cases, while the AI went on to do the work of 853 employees by November 2025. Meta abandoned the second planned wave of its Project OT cuts in 2026 after AI-assisted code changes rose 220% but shipped features rose only 36% and major incidents rose 40%, according to an internal post reported by Reuters on 26 August 2026. IBM, Duolingo and Salesforce have also scaled back or rehired against AI-replacement decisions.

The pattern is re-scoping, not abandonment. Klarna kept its automation for routine queries; IBM kept the roughly 94% of routine HR tasks it automated and found the remaining 6% resistant. What was reversed in each case was the assumption that handling a workload means replacing the people who handled its hardest part. Forrester's forecast that about half of AI-attributed layoffs will be reversed in some form by the end of 2026 resolves in January 2027, and its outcome depends heavily on what counts as a reversal, a point our predictions scoreboard records. Duolingo's chief executive described the same residual problem in a different function when reversing its 'AI-first' memo: on edge cases, 'you end up spending more effort on that than the time you saved'.

Why the reversal data is rated MEDIUM

Most aggregate figures on regret and rehiring come from Forrester, Robert Half and similar firms, which sell research, recruitment or workforce services into the market they describe. Their surveys are not random samples of employers, question wording varies, and some figures circulate through secondary reporting with slightly different values (Robert Half's rehire share appears as both 29% and 32%). We publish them because they agree in direction with the named company cases, where an executive is on the record. They should not be quoted as national statistics, and the 55% regret figure measures sentiment, not completed reversals.

3. Entry level

Software hiring recovered in 2026, but the gap for young workers in AI-exposed jobs widened to 19%

The clearest sign of AI in employment data is at the bottom of the career ladder. Stanford Digital Economy Lab researchers led by Erik Brynjolfsson, using payroll records from ADP covering millions of US workers, reported in August 2025 a 13% relative decline since late 2022 in employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, revised to 16% in November 2025 after controlling for firm-level shocks. Their August 2026 update, with data through June 2026, puts employment of that group 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. Older workers in the same occupations held steady or grew. The authors describe the result as descriptive, and find no evidence of economy-wide displacement.

Hiring data at large technology firms tells a similar story. SignalFire reported that new graduates made up about 7% of hires at large tech firms in 2024, roughly half the pre-pandemic level, and its 2026 analysis puts new-graduate hiring at 12 large firms about 65% below 2019. Industry posting analyses compiled on our software-developer page put entry-level developer postings 67% lower over 2022–2026, and 54% of companies say they stopped hiring junior developers because of AI. These are vendor figures, rated MEDIUM, and the two SignalFire numbers are different report vintages rather than one series. SignalFire also reports that engineering new-graduate hiring at those firms fell far less than design hiring, about 11% against about 48% below 2019, so the squeeze is uneven even within technology.

The overall software market moved the other way in 2026. Indeed's index of US software-development postings, set to 100 on 1 February 2020, sat near 67 at the end of both 2024 and 2025, with a low of 62.5 in March 2025. It then rose to 81.6 on 30 October 2026, still 18.4% below its pre-pandemic level but up 21% since the end of 2025. Hiring Lab reports that senior roles accounted for 71% of the gain in the year to May 2026. Total US postings were 3.8% above their February 2020 level at the same date. AI did not stop developer hiring; the recovery is concentrated in experienced roles.

Graduate hiring beyond tech is not collapsing. The National Association of Colleges and Employers' spring 2026 update projected that US employers would hire 5.6% more graduates from the class of 2026 than from the class of 2025, after projecting only 0.6% growth for the class of 2025. That projection uses a median rather than the mean NACE used in its autumn forecast, so the two are not strictly comparable. The combined reading is narrower than the headlines: entry into AI-exposed occupations is getting harder, while entry-level hiring as a whole is roughly flat to modestly up. Customer support shows the same pattern by a different route: fewer tier-1 roles means fewer people learning a product well enough to become the escalation specialists that the same organisations now depend on.

Postings are not hires

Job-posting indices measure how much hiring employers advertise, not how many people are hired. They lead actual hiring, can overstate turning points in both directions, and depend on one platform's share of the market. Indeed's software-development index counts postings classified by normalised job title, seasonally adjusted, against a 1 February 2020 baseline that was itself near a tech-hiring peak. Payroll-based studies such as Stanford's Canaries series measure employment directly but cannot isolate AI from interest rates, post-pandemic over-hiring or other shocks, which is why the authors call their results descriptive.

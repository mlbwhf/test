5. Exposure and productivity

Exposure is highest in translation, administrative and legal work, but measured productivity gains across whole jobs remain small

Exposure estimates rank occupations by how much of their work current AI could do. The IMF estimated in January 2024 that about 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, rising to about 60% in advanced economies, where more work is cognitive. At the occupational level, the studies compiled on our by-occupation page put interpreters and translators above 98% automation exposure, market research at 53% of analyst tasks, office and administrative support at a 46% task-automation share and legal work at 44%. These come from different studies with different methods; they are a ranking, not a set of comparable measurements. Translation is the clearest measured case: machine translation crossed the threshold of commercial acceptability for routine content, and surviving demand is concentrated in legal, medical and certified work where accuracy carries liability.

The least exposed work is defined less by industry than by the content of the job. Roles where judgement under ambiguity, personal accountability, physical presence or human trust is the product sit near the bottom of every exposure ranking. Payroll data reflect the split: Stanford's August 2026 update reports that employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the three least-exposed occupational quintiles grew about 10% over the period studied, while it fell about 11% in the most exposed. Exposure is also a poor predictor of headcount. Office and administrative support has the highest task share yet not the highest observed job loss, because the remaining 54% of tasks still needs accountable people. IBM's HR automation is the standard example: it automated about 94% of routine tasks and then moved to triple US entry-level hiring because the residual required judgement.

Measured productivity effects are smaller than exposure estimates imply. The St. Louis Fed's Real-Time Population Survey put time saved by generative AI at about 1.4% of all US work hours in November 2024, rising to about 2% by the fourth quarter of 2025 and about 2.2% in the first quarter of 2026; workers who use AI report saving about 5.4% of their own hours. These are self-reports, and saved time raises output only if it is redeployed. Controlled studies are mixed: a 2023 field study of customer-support agents found 14% more issues resolved per hour, while METR's randomised trial, published in July 2025, found experienced developers took 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools.

Firms report little effect so far. In the NBER study 'Firm Data on AI', published in February 2026 and based on surveys of about 6,000 executives in the US, UK, Germany and Australia, more than 90% reported no effect of AI on their firm's employment over the previous three years and 89% reported none on labour productivity. The same executives expect a 1.4% productivity gain and a 0.7% employment decline over the next three years. Inside software teams, the gains in output are real but partly offset by verification: survey data put weekly time spent reviewing AI-generated code at 11.4 hours in the first quarter of 2026, against 9.8 hours writing new code.

Customer support, the first function automated at scale, shows the same gap between capability and replacement. Across enterprise programmes in 2026, the median share of tier-1 contacts resolved by AI without a human was 41.2%, with the top quartile at 58.7% and the bottom at 22.4%, well below the 70–90% that vendors advertise. Simple, lookup-based requests such as refunds and password resets deflect at 65–80%; nuanced complaints rarely exceed 25%. Re-contact rates were 11.3% on AI-resolved tickets against 8.7% for human-resolved ones, so part of the measured saving reappears later as repeat work. Satisfaction was highest, at about 89%, where AI triaged and humans handled escalations, against about 74% for AI alone. These are 2026 vendor benchmarks, rated MEDIUM, and deflection is defined differently by different vendors.

Exposure is not job loss

An exposure estimate measures how much of an occupation's work current AI could perform or assist, not how many jobs will disappear. A job is a bundle of tasks; when part of the bundle is automated, the role usually changes shape and loses some headcount rather than vanishing, and the surviving work concentrates in judgement, exceptions and accountability. Task-automation share, whole-role exposure and projected headcount reduction are three different measures, and studies use different methods and time horizons. Treat any single exposure percentage as a position in a ranking, not as a forecast of jobs lost.

Machine-Learning Models Compared: We Ran 13 Algorithms on 19 Datasets

A viral clip ranked nine models on one unnamed dataset. We ran 13, from Naive Bayes to the TabPFN transformer, on 19 public benchmark datasets with five random splits each, to show what wins, by how much, and how much the answer depends on how you test.

  • Report AI ran 13 machine-learning classifiers on 19 public tabular datasets from the Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022) benchmark, with 1,000 training rows and five random splits per dataset, in November 2026.

Analysis

Rankings of machine-learning models are everywhere, and most are built on one dataset and one random split. We wanted to know how much of such a ranking is signal. So we ran 13 widely used classifiers, from Naive Bayes and logistic regression to gradient-boosted trees and the TabPFN tabular transformer, on 19 datasets that researchers use precisely because they are hard, and repeated every comparison on five random splits.

The headline is clear but narrower than viral charts suggest. TabPFN v2 finished first on average with 76.7% accuracy, 1.0 points ahead of CatBoost. Below the top, gradient-boosted trees and random forests sit within a point or two of each other, and the order among them changes from dataset to dataset. Simple models are not useless: in a 10-split comparison of the other 12 models, logistic regression won outright on 2 datasets. But single trees, k-nearest neighbours and Naive Bayes trail by a wide margin on these tasks.

How you test changes what you see. On a single dataset with a single split, even a model as dominant as TabPFN v2 came first in 80.0% of runs, and 7 different models won at least once; without it, the best of the other 12 models topped only 55.3% of single runs. Ranking by ROC AUC instead of accuracy moved Random forest by 2 places. Turning off feature scaling cost SVM (RBF) 7.9 points of accuracy. Making the positive class rare (10%) pushed every model's accuracy towards the 90.0% a do-nothing majority-class guess scores, while balanced accuracy exposed which ones actually learned.

The literature agrees with the shape of our results and adds a time dimension. Tree ensembles beat deep networks on medium-sized tables in the 2021–23 studies; since 2025, pretrained tabular foundation models such as TabPFN have taken the top of the TabArena leaderboard, while classic tree models remain the fast, cheap and robust default. The models that decide what people see online, from ad clicks to feeds and spam filters, span the whole range, from logistic regression to trillion-parameter transformers.

Key takeaways
  • On these 19 benchmark datasets at 1,000 training rows, TabPFN v2 ranked first and boosted trees and random forests filled most of the top half.
  • Never trust a ranking from one dataset and one split: the winner changed in a large share of single runs.
  • Accuracy alone hides problems; check ROC AUC, and balanced accuracy when one class is rare.
  • Scale features before using k-nearest neighbours, SVMs, neural networks or logistic regression.
  • TabPFN's newer, stronger versions are non-commercial; the openly licensed v2 used here is capped at 10,000 rows.

2. The ranking

TabPFN v2 finished first; boosted trees and random forests filled most of the top half

Each model was trained with its library's default settings on 1,000 rows and tested on 2,000 different rows, five times per dataset with fresh random splits. All 19 tasks are balanced yes/no problems, so 50% accuracy is chance. We rank models by their average rank across datasets, the standard way to compare learners across tasks of different difficulty, and also report mean accuracy, ROC AUC and wins.

TabPFN v2 averaged 76.7% accuracy and a mean rank of 1.32. CatBoost followed at 75.7%. The fastest model, Naive Bayes, took a median 2.1 milliseconds to train and predict; the slowest, TabPFN v2, took 49.3 seconds on our 4-core CPU.

3. Dataset by dataset

The winner depends on the dataset, and margins are often under a point

The datasets come from the benchmark that Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux assembled for NeurIPS 2022, published on Hugging Face as inria-soda/tabular-benchmark. They range from 3,434 to 940,160 rows and from 7 to 419 features, covering credit, medicine, physics, housing and public records.

One caveat favours complex models: the benchmark's authors removed datasets that were 'too easy', where a linear model or a single tree came within 5% of a neural network and of gradient boosting. Logistic regression and decision trees therefore look worse here than they would on a random sample of business datasets.

4. How the test changes what you see

One dataset and one split crowned 7 different winners

A chart built from a single dataset and a single random split, like the viral one, is one draw from a noisy process. Across our 95 single runs, 7 different models came first at least once. The overall winner, TabPFN v2, was first in 80.0% of runs, and the average gap between first and second place in a single run was just 0.9 points. The agreement between a single run's ranking and the overall ranking (Kendall's tau) averaged 0.68, where 1 would mean identical.

The metric matters too. Accuracy counts right answers at one threshold; ROC AUC measures how well a model orders cases from least to most likely, across all thresholds. Switching from accuracy to AUC moved Random forest 2 places and Extra trees 2.

The effect is stronger when no model dominates. Re-running the comparison without TabPFN, on 10 splits per dataset (190 single runs), 9 different models came first at least once, and the best of the 12, CatBoost, topped only 55.3% of single runs. A one-dataset, one-split chart has roughly the reliability of a coin toss weighted towards the favourite.

5. Training-set size

With 100 rows and with 10,000, different models lead

We repeated the test with 100, 300, 1,000, 3,000 and 10,000 training rows on the 13 datasets large enough for every size. Accuracy rises for every model as data grows, but not equally: models that rely on strong built-in assumptions do relatively well with very little data, while flexible ensembles and neural networks gain most from more rows. TabPFN v2 led at every size it was run (up to 1,000 rows); Naive Bayes was the only model whose accuracy fell as data grew, from 63.0% at 100 rows to 59.0% at 10,000, because more data cannot fix its wrong independence assumption.

6. Scaling, imbalance and tuning

Three setup choices change the scoreboard as much as the choice of model

Four models measure distances or weighted sums directly on the raw features, so a feature measured in thousands drowns one measured in fractions unless the data is standardised first. Removing that one preprocessing line changed their accuracy by: SVM (RBF) -7.9 points; Neural network (MLP) -6.4 points; k-nearest neighbours -5.6 points; Logistic regression -1.0 points.

When only 10% of cases are positive, a model that always answers 'no' scores 90.0% accuracy while learning nothing; its balanced accuracy is 50.0%. Of the 12 real models, 9 scored within two points above that do-nothing baseline and 3 (Neural network (MLP), Decision tree, Naive Bayes) scored below it, yet their balanced accuracy ranged between 55.6% and 63.6% balanced accuracy. The leader changes with the metric: CatBoost had the highest accuracy, Neural network (MLP) the highest balanced accuracy. On imbalanced data, an accuracy figure is meaningless without the base rate. (TabPFN was not run in this test.)

Default settings are not the whole story. A modest search over 20 hyperparameter configurations, scored by 3-fold cross-validation, helped Decision tree most (+3.4 points) and moved XGBoost to the top of the nine tuned models, from 3rd at default settings. McElfresh et al. (2023) reached the same conclusion at larger scale: light tuning of gradient-boosted trees often matters more than the choice between model families.

7. What the research says

Trees led until 2023; pretrained tabular transformers lead the 2026 leaderboards

Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (arXiv July 2022; NeurIPS 2022) benchmarked tree-based models against deep learning on 45 tabular datasets and found that tree-based models such as XGBoost and random forests remain state-of-the-art on medium-sized data of about 10,000 samples. McElfresh et al. (2023) found that the neural-net-versus-boosted-tree debate is overemphasised: for many datasets the gap is negligible, or light hyperparameter tuning of a GBDT matters more than the choice between model families.

On TabArena's live leaderboard (default view: single models, 51 datasets, data regenerated 11 November 2026), the top five entries are NVIDIA's Kumo-Tabular (Elo 1971), LimiX-2 (1959), Kumo-Tabular-Medium (1909), Neuralk-AI's Seldon (1903) and Prior Labs' TabPFN-3.5 (1868), all tabular foundation models run with default settings. On TabArena's default leaderboard (11 November 2026), classic models rank far below: tuned-and-ensembled CatBoost scores Elo 1402, XGBoost 1360, RandomForest 1172, k-nearest neighbours 993 and linear models 990, against 1971 for the leader.

Our results sit between these: at 1,000 training rows and default settings, without the ensembling and long tuning budgets of the leaderboards, TabPFN v2 led, and the gaps among the top tree ensembles were small.

8. How these models shape what you see

From spam filters to feeds, every model family in this test decides something people see

The same families compete inside the systems that choose what appears on screens. Early spam filters were Naive Bayes classifiers; ad-click prediction at Facebook in 2014 combined boosted trees with logistic regression; feeds and video recommendations moved to deep neural networks and, by 2024–26, to transformers with billions or trillions of parameters. The model decides the ranking, but the weights a company puts on each predicted action (a like, a reply, a report) decide what the ranking rewards.

X's For You README (update of 14 August 2026) warns that action weights scale each viewer's predicted probability of an action, so a report weighted 468 times a like does not mean one report cancels 468 likes.

Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“TabPFN scores 79.5%, a neural network 74.0% and Naive Bayes 42.1%.”These figures come from a short AI-labelled video that names no dataset; they cannot be checked. Accuracy is a property of a model on a dataset, not of a model. On Report AI's 19-dataset benchmark the order of the middle of the table differs.
“Trees always beat neural networks on tabular data.”That held in 2021–23 studies of tuned MLP-style networks on medium-sized data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; McElfresh et al., 2023). By November 2026, pretrained tabular foundation models held the top 21 places of the TabArena leaderboard, while tuned gradient-boosted trees remained strong and far cheaper.
“TabPFN is open source.”The code and the TabPFN v2 weights are openly licensed (Apache 2.0 with an attribution clause). The weights of TabPFN-2.5, 2.6, 3 and 3.5 are released under non-commercial licences; production use needs a commercial licence from Prior Labs.
“The No Free Lunch theorem means every algorithm is equally good.”Wolpert's 1996 theorems average over all possible problems. Real-world data has structure, which is why some model families win consistently on real benchmarks.
Definitions

Terms used in this report

Accuracy
The share of test cases a model classifies correctly at its default decision threshold. On a balanced two-class problem, guessing scores 50%.
ROC AUC
The probability that the model scores a randomly chosen positive case above a randomly chosen negative one; 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect. It ignores the decision threshold.
Balanced accuracy
The average of the accuracy on each class. It stops a model that always predicts the majority class from looking good on imbalanced data.
Train/test split
Rows used to fit the model are kept apart from rows used to score it. Repeating with several random splits shows how much a result depends on the luck of the draw.
Gradient boosting
An ensemble that adds decision trees one at a time, each fitted to the errors of the trees before it. CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost and HistGB are implementations.
Tabular foundation model
A model pretrained on many (often synthetic) tables that predicts on a new table from its rows as context, without conventional training on it. TabPFN is the best-known example.
Benchmark
A fixed set of tasks and a scoring rule used to compare models. Results hold for the benchmark's tasks, not for every problem.

Frequently asked

Which machine-learning model is the most accurate?

There is no single most accurate model; it depends on the data. On Report AI's November 2026 benchmark of 13 classifiers on 19 public tabular datasets with 1,000 training rows, TabPFN v2 had the highest mean accuracy (76.7%) and won 17 of 19 datasets, ahead of CatBoost (75.7%).

Do neural networks beat decision trees?

A single decision tree is one of the weakest models, but ensembles of trees are among the strongest. In Report AI's benchmark a default multilayer-perceptron neural network ranked 9 of 13 and a single decision tree 12, while gradient-boosted tree ensembles filled most of the top half. Pretrained tabular transformers such as TabPFN now lead the TabArena leaderboard.

What is TabPFN?

TabPFN is a transformer pretrained on millions of synthetic datasets that predicts on a new table in a single forward pass. Hollmann et al. described version 2 in Nature in January 2025 and reported that it outperforms previous methods on datasets of up to 10,000 samples. Later versions from Prior Labs handle up to 1 million rows but are licensed for non-commercial use only.

Why do machine-learning rankings disagree?

Because results depend on the dataset, the random split, the metric, the amount of training data, preprocessing and tuning. In Report AI's benchmark, 7 different models won at least one of 95 single dataset-and-split runs, and the overall winner was first in only 80.0% of them.

Which algorithms decide what I see online?

Many. Facebook described combining boosted decision trees with logistic regression to predict ad clicks in 2014; YouTube used deep neural networks for recommendations in 2016; X's For You feed is ranked by a transformer model according to its open-source repository (November 2026); and Naive Bayes classifiers were the first academic spam filters (1998).

Methodology & sources

Figures are compiled from company disclosures, regulators, research firms and reputable reporting, and cross-checked against Report AI's own index pages. Each value carries its as-of date, source and a confidence rating: HIGH where the primary source was read, MEDIUM where it rests on consistent reputable secondary reporting (most figures, because many primary pages cannot be opened from our research environment), DERIVED where it is our own arithmetic, and CONTESTED where credible sources disagree. A year means the calendar year the figure describes; 2026 values are the latest available and marked “to date”. Weekly and monthly user counts, and forecasts and outcomes, are never compared as if they were the same measure. Report AI's benchmark used 19 binary classification datasets from the Grinsztajn et al. (2022) tabular benchmark (Hugging Face inria-soda/tabular-benchmark; mixed-feature versions where available), with classes balanced by the benchmark's authors. For each dataset and random seed we drew 1,000 training and 2,000 test rows (stratified), trained each model with library defaults (scikit-learn 1.9.1, XGBoost 3.2.0, LightGBM 4.7.0, CatBoost 1.2.10, TabPFN v2 weights via the tabpfn 10.0.0 package), standardised features for the four scale-sensitive models, and recorded accuracy, balanced accuracy, ROC AUC and time on a 4-core CPU without a GPU. The main ranking uses five splits per dataset for all 13 models (TabPFN costs 25–80 seconds per fit on a CPU); a 10-split run of the other 12 models is reported as a robustness check. Further experiments varied training size (100 to 10,000 rows, 3 seeds, TabPFN to 1,000 rows, on the 13 datasets with at least 6,000 rows per class), feature scaling (10 seeds), class balance (10% positives, 3 seeds, without TabPFN) and hyperparameter tuning (20-configuration random search with 3-fold cross-validation, 3 seeds, nine models). Categorical features were left in the benchmark's ordinal coding. All averages, ranks and shares are Report AI calculations (DERIVED); literature figures carry their source and confidence.

Analysis

There is no single official way to categorise large language models. Regulators sort them by training compute, researchers by architecture, buyers by price and openness, and governments by origin. Report AI's taxonomy keeps those views separate rather than collapsing them into one ranking, because the same model sits in different boxes depending on the question: DeepSeek-V4-Pro is a frontier-class open-weight mixture-of-experts reasoning model from China, and it is below the EU AI Act's 10²⁵ FLOP presumption on Epoch AI's estimate.

The table shows three shifts since 2023. First, reasoning: models that spend extra compute thinking before they answer went from one model in our 2023–24 rows (OpenAI's o1, December 2024) to 67.2% of 2025–26 releases; on Artificial Analysis's index, 108 (95.6%) of indexed variants are now reasoning variants. Second, sparsity: mixture-of-experts layers, which let a model hold far more parameters than it uses for each token, moved from a minority to most open-weight releases. Third, openness diverged: open-weight models now come from every region, but the closed frontier still leads on capability benchmarks, by about 18 points on Artificial Analysis's index in mid-November 2026.

Parameter counts have become a poor guide to cost and capability. Kimi K3.1 Pro has 2,780 billion total parameters but activates 104 billion per token; DeepSeek-V4-Pro has 1.6 trillion total and 49 billion active, yet Epoch AI puts its training compute below 10²⁵ FLOP. The four models Epoch estimates above 10²⁶ FLOP (GPT-6 Astra, Grok 4, GPT-4.5 and Grok 3) are all closed and have no published parameter count. For readers, the useful numbers are active parameters (cost per token), context window (how much it can read), and whether it reasons (speed versus depth).

The categories matter because they change what a user sees. A reasoning model answers a hard maths question correctly more often but takes longer and bills for hidden reasoning tokens. A small on-device model keeps data on the phone but knows less. An open-weight model can be audited and run privately, but under licence terms. The same chatbot interface can sit on top of any of these, which is why the label on the model card matters more than the brand on the app.

Key takeaways
  • Classify a model on several dimensions at once; no single label ("open", "frontier", "small") describes it.
  • Compare mixture-of-experts models on active parameters, not total parameters.
  • "Open-weight" is not "open-source": only models that also release training code and data (OLMo, Pythia, Apertus) meet the Open Source Initiative's 2024 definition.
  • Reasoning is now the default for new releases; expect slower, more expensive and more accurate answers on hard tasks.
  • Compute thresholds, not parameter counts, decide regulatory duties: 10²⁵ FLOP in the EU, 10²⁶ in California and New York.
1. The eight dimensions

Eight questions place any LLM: how it is built, how it thinks, who can use it, and who made it

Each dimension answers a different practical question. The table below lists them with the number of models in Report AI's table that fall in each category. Counts describe our selection of notable models, not every model released, so they show direction rather than market shares.

Two dimensions need care. Training stage is a property of a release, not a family: Llama 3.1 405B exists as a base model and as an instruction-tuned model. And openness is a spectrum: licences range from Apache 2.0 to custom terms with revenue caps, attribution rules or non-commercial clauses.

2. Architecture

Mixture-of-experts overtook dense transformers among open-weight models, and hybrids are spreading

Almost every model in the table is a decoder-only transformer, but they differ in how the layers are arranged. In the table, 28 models are mixture-of-experts, 26 are dense, 10 are hybrids that replace most attention layers with state-space or linear-attention layers, and 4 are diffusion language models that refine a whole draft in parallel instead of writing token by token. The remaining 25 are closed models whose architecture is not disclosed.

Among open-weight models, mixture-of-experts went from 5 of 18 releases in 2023–24 to 27 of 43 in 2025–26. The reason is cost: only the active experts run for each token, so a model can hold a large amount of knowledge while charging compute like a much smaller one.

Architecture categories

CategoryDefinitionExamples
Dense decoder-only transformerA stack of Transformer decoder blocks in which every parameter is used for every token, trained to predict the next token from the left context. The Transformer itself replaced recurrence with attention (Vaswani et al., 2017); GPT-4's technical report describes 'a Transformer-based model pre-trained to predict the next token', and Meta describes Llama 3.1 405B as 'a dense Transformer with 405B parameters'. For a dense model, total parameters equal parameters used per token, so size and per-token compute move together.Llama 3.1 405B, Qwen3-32B, Gemma 3 27B, OLMo 2 32B, Phi-4, Apertus 70B
Mixture-of-experts (MoE)Feed-forward layers are split into many 'experts' and a learned router sends each token to a few of them, so only a fraction of the weights run per token (Shazeer et al., 2017; Switch Transformer, 2021). Two numbers therefore matter: total parameters (memory needed to hold the model) and active parameters (compute per token). Mixtral 8x7B has 47B total but 'only uses 13B active parameters'; DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total and 37B activated; Kimi K3.1 Pro has 2.8T total and 104B activated. Comparing a MoE's total count with a dense model's count overstates its per-token cost.Mixtral 8x7B, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-235B-A22B, gpt-oss-120b, Llama 4 Maverick, Mistral Large 4
Hybrid / state-space / linear attentionModels that replace most quadratic self-attention layers with layers whose cost grows linearly with sequence length — selective state-space (Mamba) layers or linear-attention variants such as Gated DeltaNet, lightning attention or Kimi Delta Attention — keeping a few full-attention layers for exact recall. Mamba introduced selective state spaces (Gu & Dao, 2023); Jamba interleaved Transformer and Mamba blocks with MoE; Nemotron-H 'replace[s] the majority of self-attention layers… with Mamba layers'; Falcon-H1 runs attention and SSM heads in parallel; Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.8 use three Gated DeltaNet layers per gated-attention layer. The payoff is cheaper long-context inference. Sliding-window or compressed-attention schemes (Gemma, DeepSeek-V4) are still attention and are not counted here.Jamba 1.5 Large, Nemotron-H 56B, Falcon-H1-34B, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, MiniMax-M1, Granite 4.0-H-Small, Kimi K3.1 Pro
Diffusion language modelInstead of emitting one token at a time left to right, the model starts from masked or noised text and refines many positions in parallel over several denoising steps. LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025) trained an 8B masked-diffusion model 'from scratch under the pre-training and SFT paradigm'; Inception's Mercury Coder is a commercial diffusion LLM reporting 1,109 tokens/s; Google showed Gemini Diffusion at I/O (May 2025) and released DiffusionGemma 26B A4B with open weights (June 2026), which denoises 256 tokens at a time. The main selling point is generation speed.LLaDA 8B, Mercury Coder, Gemini Diffusion, DiffusionGemma 26B A4B
Encoder-only and encoder–decoder (for contrast)Pre-LLM-era Transformer designs that are not generative chat models in the modern sense. BERT is encoder-only: it reads the whole input bidirectionally ('jointly conditioning on both left and right context') and is fine-tuned for classification, retrieval or extraction. T5 is encoder–decoder: it 'converts all text-based language problems into a text-to-text format'. They survive in embeddings, rerankers and some translation systems; none of the generative models in the table uses them.BERT (2018), T5 (2019)
UndisclosedClosed developers usually do not publish architecture or size. 3. Training stage and reasoning

Reasoning went from one model to two-thirds of new releases in two years

A base model is the raw result of pretraining: it continues text but does not follow instructions. Instruction tuning and preference training turn it into a chat model. Reasoning models are trained, mostly with reinforcement learning, to write out a chain of intermediate steps before the final answer, spending more compute at inference time. Hybrid models let the user or the application switch that thinking off or cap its budget.

In the table, 25 models always reason, 19 are hybrids and 47 do not reason; 0 could not be verified. By release year, the share with a reasoning mode was 2023: 0 of 8, 2024: 1 of 19, 2025: 22 of 43, 2026: 21 of 21.

4. Openness and licences

61 of 93 models have downloadable weights, but only 4 meet the open-source definition

Openness is the dimension most often mislabelled. In the table, 32 models are closed (available only through an API or app), 57 are open-weight (the trained weights can be downloaded under a licence) and 4 are open-source in the sense of the Open Source Initiative's Open Source AI Definition 1.0 (October 2024), which also requires the training code and enough information about the data to rebuild the model.

Licences differ widely. Of the models with downloadable weights, 29 use permissive licences such as Apache 2.0 or MIT, 24 use custom licences with conditions (attribution, acceptable-use rules, caps on very large commercial users) and 4 are non-commercial. The share of table models with downloadable weights by release year was 2023: 5 of 8, 2024: 13 of 19, 2025: 29 of 43, 2026: 14 of 23.

On Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index v5.0.1 (read 15 Nov 2026), the best proprietary model, Claude Fable 5.2 at max effort, scored 64.6 and the best open-weights model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, scored 46.5 — a gap of about 18 points. Of the 113 model variants with an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score on 15 Nov 2026, 44 (38.9%) are open-weights and 108 (95.6%) are reasoning variants.

5. Modality, size and specialisation

Most new models read images, and a small-model tier now runs on phones

In the table, 50 models take text only and 41 also take images and sometimes audio or video; 1 is an omni model that speaks and listens natively. Image output is usually handled by a separate model and is defined here but not counted.

By size and deployment, 32 models are frontier, 6 are closed efficient tiers of undisclosed size, 23 are large (100 billion parameters or more, below the frontier), 24 are mid-size, 4 are small (under 10 billion) and 4 are built to run on phones or laptops. There is no regulatory definition of a small language model; the 10-billion line follows NVIDIA researchers' 2025 working definition.

Context windows range widely. The longest advertised in the table is Llama 4 Scout's 10,000,000 tokens (2025-04); 1 million tokens is the most common figure among 2026 flagships. Advertised windows are maxima: accuracy on information buried deep in a long prompt is typically lower than at the start.

6. Training compute and regulation

Only 4 models are estimated above 10²⁶ FLOP, and all are closed

Epoch AI's AI-models database, downloaded 15 Nov 2026, holds 28 entries with a training-compute estimate of at least 10²⁵ FLOP (27 distinct models; GPT-4 appears twice), of which 8 are open-weight and 4 exceed 10²⁶ FLOP — Grok 3, GPT-4.5, Grok 4 and GPT-6 Astra, all closed.

Epoch AI's 'Over 30 AI models have been trained at the scale of GPT-4' data insight (updated 6 Jun 2025; CSV updated 21 Nov 2025) lists 33 publicly disclosed models estimated above 10²⁵ FLOP from 12 developers, of which 3 have open weights (Llama 3.1-405B, Mistral Large 2, Nemotron-4 340B).

Compute is what regulators count. The EU AI Act presumes a general-purpose model has systemic risk above 10²⁵ FLOP; California's SB 53 and New York's RAISE Act use 10²⁶. The United States' federal 10²⁶ reporting threshold from Executive Order 14110 was rescinded in January 2025. Epoch AI has no estimate for most closed flagships released in 2025–26, so any count above these thresholds undercounts the newest models.

Definitions

Terms used in this index

Large language model
A neural network, almost always a transformer, trained on very large text corpora to predict the next token, then usually tuned to follow instructions.
Total vs active parameters
Total parameters are all the weights a model holds (and so the memory needed to serve it). In a mixture-of-experts model only a subset, the active parameters, runs for each token, which sets the compute cost per token.
Reasoning model
A model trained to generate intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, spending more inference-time compute on harder problems. A hybrid model lets that reasoning be switched off or capped.
Open-weight vs open-source
Open-weight: the trained weights are downloadable under a licence. Open-source AI (OSI definition, 2024): weights plus training code and sufficient information about the training data, under terms that allow use, study, modification and sharing.
State-space / linear-attention hybrid
An architecture that replaces most full-attention layers with layers whose cost grows linearly with sequence length (Mamba, Gated DeltaNet, Kimi Delta Attention), keeping a few attention layers for recall.
Diffusion language model
A model that generates by iteratively refining a full draft of masked tokens in parallel, rather than writing one token after another.
Context window
The maximum number of tokens a model can take in one request, prompt and conversation history included.
Frontier model
A model at or near the leading edge of capability and training compute at its release; this index uses Epoch AI's top-5-by-compute flag, a 10²⁵ FLOP estimate, or a top-tier closed flagship of a lab with such a run on record.

Frequently asked

How are large language models categorized?

Report AI classifies LLMs on eight dimensions: architecture (dense, mixture-of-experts, hybrid, diffusion), training stage and reasoning, openness (closed, open-weight, open-source), modality, size and deployment, specialisation, training-compute class and origin. Its November 2026 table places 93 notable models on each.

What is the difference between an open-weight and an open-source LLM?

An open-weight model's trained weights can be downloaded under a licence, but its training data and full code usually are not released. An open-source model under the Open Source Initiative's 2024 definition also releases the training code and enough information about the data to rebuild it; OLMo, Pythia and Apertus qualify, Llama, DeepSeek and Qwen do not.

What is a mixture-of-experts model?

A mixture-of-experts model splits its feed-forward layers into many experts and routes each token to a few of them, so only a fraction of its parameters runs per token. In Report AI's table, 27 of 43 open-weight models released in 2025–26 use the design; Kimi K3.1 Pro has 2,780 billion total and 104 billion active parameters.

How many LLMs are reasoning models?

In Report AI's table, 43 of 64 models released in 2025–26 (67.2%) have a reasoning mode, against 1 of 27 released in 2023–24. On Artificial Analysis's index in November 2026, 108 of 113 indexed model variants were reasoning variants.

Which LLMs were trained with the most compute?

On Epoch AI's estimates (database downloaded 15 November 2026), four models exceed 10²⁶ FLOP: GPT-6 Astra (9.4 × 10²⁶), Grok 4 (5 × 10²⁶), GPT-4.5 (3.8 × 10²⁶) and Grok 3 (3.5 × 10²⁶). All are closed. Epoch has no estimate for most closed flagships released in 2025–26.

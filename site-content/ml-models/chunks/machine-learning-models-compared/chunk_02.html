Analysis

Rankings of machine-learning models are everywhere, and most are built on one dataset and one random split. We wanted to know how much of such a ranking is signal. So we ran 13 widely used classifiers, from Naive Bayes and logistic regression to gradient-boosted trees and the TabPFN tabular transformer, on 19 datasets that researchers use precisely because they are hard, and repeated every comparison on five random splits.

The headline is clear but narrower than viral charts suggest. TabPFN v2 finished first on average with 76.7% accuracy, 1.0 points ahead of CatBoost. Below the top, gradient-boosted trees and random forests sit within a point or two of each other, and the order among them changes from dataset to dataset. Simple models are not useless: in a 10-split comparison of the other 12 models, logistic regression won outright on 2 datasets. But single trees, k-nearest neighbours and Naive Bayes trail by a wide margin on these tasks.

How you test changes what you see. On a single dataset with a single split, even a model as dominant as TabPFN v2 came first in 80.0% of runs, and 7 different models won at least once; without it, the best of the other 12 models topped only 55.3% of single runs. Ranking by ROC AUC instead of accuracy moved Random forest by 2 places. Turning off feature scaling cost SVM (RBF) 7.9 points of accuracy. Making the positive class rare (10%) pushed every model's accuracy towards the 90.0% a do-nothing majority-class guess scores, while balanced accuracy exposed which ones actually learned.

The literature agrees with the shape of our results and adds a time dimension. Tree ensembles beat deep networks on medium-sized tables in the 2021–23 studies; since 2025, pretrained tabular foundation models such as TabPFN have taken the top of the TabArena leaderboard, while classic tree models remain the fast, cheap and robust default. The models that decide what people see online, from ad clicks to feeds and spam filters, span the whole range, from logistic regression to trillion-parameter transformers.

Key takeaways
  • On these 19 benchmark datasets at 1,000 training rows, TabPFN v2 ranked first and boosted trees and random forests filled most of the top half.
  • Never trust a ranking from one dataset and one split: the winner changed in a large share of single runs.
  • Accuracy alone hides problems; check ROC AUC, and balanced accuracy when one class is rare.
  • Scale features before using k-nearest neighbours, SVMs, neural networks or logistic regression.
  • TabPFN's newer, stronger versions are non-commercial; the openly licensed v2 used here is capped at 10,000 rows.
1. The viral chart

A nine-model chart from one unnamed dataset cannot tell you which model is best

The chart that prompted this report is a short video captioned '13 machine learning models in 60 seconds', labelled as AI content. It shows nine of the thirteen: TabPFN 79.5%, a neural network 74.0%, random forest 73.5%, gradient boosting 72.7%, a support vector machine 72.0%, k-nearest neighbours 68.3%, a decision tree 64.1%, logistic regression 59.3% and Naive Bayes 42.1%.

Report AI could not find the original video or the dataset behind it, so its figures cannot be checked or reproduced. A Naive Bayes score of 42.1% is below the 50% a coin toss scores on a balanced two-class problem, which suggests a multi-class task or an unusual split. The table compares the video's order with our averages over many datasets. The top agrees: TabPFN ranked first in our benchmark too. Below it the order differs; the video's second-placed neural network ranked 5th of the same nine models in our test, a sign that the middle of the pack depends on the data.

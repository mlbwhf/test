8. How these models shape what you see

From spam filters to feeds, every model family in this test decides something people see

The same families compete inside the systems that choose what appears on screens. Early spam filters were Naive Bayes classifiers; ad-click prediction at Facebook in 2014 combined boosted trees with logistic regression; feeds and video recommendations moved to deep neural networks and, by 2024–26, to transformers with billions or trillions of parameters. The model decides the ranking, but the weights a company puts on each predicted action (a like, a reply, a report) decide what the ranking rewards.

X's For You README (update of 14 August 2026) warns that action weights scale each viewer's predicted probability of an action, so a report weighted 468 times a like does not mean one report cancels 468 likes.

Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“TabPFN scores 79.5%, a neural network 74.0% and Naive Bayes 42.1%.”These figures come from a short AI-labelled video that names no dataset; they cannot be checked. Accuracy is a property of a model on a dataset, not of a model. On Report AI's 19-dataset benchmark the order of the middle of the table differs.
“Trees always beat neural networks on tabular data.”That held in 2021–23 studies of tuned MLP-style networks on medium-sized data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; McElfresh et al., 2023). By November 2026, pretrained tabular foundation models held the top 21 places of the TabArena leaderboard, while tuned gradient-boosted trees remained strong and far cheaper.
“TabPFN is open source.”The code and the TabPFN v2 weights are openly licensed (Apache 2.0 with an attribution clause). The weights of TabPFN-2.5, 2.6, 3 and 3.5 are released under non-commercial licences; production use needs a commercial licence from Prior Labs.
“The No Free Lunch theorem means every algorithm is equally good.”Wolpert's 1996 theorems average over all possible problems. Real-world data has structure, which is why some model families win consistently on real benchmarks.
Definitions

Terms used in this report

Accuracy
The share of test cases a model classifies correctly at its default decision threshold. On a balanced two-class problem, guessing scores 50%.
ROC AUC
The probability that the model scores a randomly chosen positive case above a randomly chosen negative one; 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect. It ignores the decision threshold.
Balanced accuracy
The average of the accuracy on each class. It stops a model that always predicts the majority class from looking good on imbalanced data.
Train/test split
Rows used to fit the model are kept apart from rows used to score it. Repeating with several random splits shows how much a result depends on the luck of the draw.
Gradient boosting
An ensemble that adds decision trees one at a time, each fitted to the errors of the trees before it. CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost and HistGB are implementations.
Tabular foundation model
A model pretrained on many (often synthetic) tables that predicts on a new table from its rows as context, without conventional training on it. TabPFN is the best-known example.
Benchmark
A fixed set of tasks and a scoring rule used to compare models. Results hold for the benchmark's tasks, not for every problem.

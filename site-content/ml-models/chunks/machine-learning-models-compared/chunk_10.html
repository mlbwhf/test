Frequently asked

Which machine-learning model is the most accurate?

There is no single most accurate model; it depends on the data. On Report AI's November 2026 benchmark of 13 classifiers on 19 public tabular datasets with 1,000 training rows, TabPFN v2 had the highest mean accuracy (76.7%) and won 17 of 19 datasets, ahead of CatBoost (75.7%).

Do neural networks beat decision trees?

A single decision tree is one of the weakest models, but ensembles of trees are among the strongest. In Report AI's benchmark a default multilayer-perceptron neural network ranked 9 of 13 and a single decision tree 12, while gradient-boosted tree ensembles filled most of the top half. Pretrained tabular transformers such as TabPFN now lead the TabArena leaderboard.

What is TabPFN?

TabPFN is a transformer pretrained on millions of synthetic datasets that predicts on a new table in a single forward pass. Hollmann et al. described version 2 in Nature in January 2025 and reported that it outperforms previous methods on datasets of up to 10,000 samples. Later versions from Prior Labs handle up to 1 million rows but are licensed for non-commercial use only.

Why do machine-learning rankings disagree?

Because results depend on the dataset, the random split, the metric, the amount of training data, preprocessing and tuning. In Report AI's benchmark, 7 different models won at least one of 95 single dataset-and-split runs, and the overall winner was first in only 80.0% of them.

Which algorithms decide what I see online?

Many. Facebook described combining boosted decision trees with logistic regression to predict ad clicks in 2014; YouTube used deep neural networks for recommendations in 2016; X's For You feed is ranked by a transformer model according to its open-source repository (November 2026); and Naive Bayes classifiers were the first academic spam filters (1998).

Methodology & sources

Figures are compiled from company disclosures, regulators, research firms and reputable reporting, and cross-checked against Report AI's own index pages. Each value carries its as-of date, source and a confidence rating: HIGH where the primary source was read, MEDIUM where it rests on consistent reputable secondary reporting (most figures, because many primary pages cannot be opened from our research environment), DERIVED where it is our own arithmetic, and CONTESTED where credible sources disagree. A year means the calendar year the figure describes; 2026 values are the latest available and marked “to date”. Weekly and monthly user counts, and forecasts and outcomes, are never compared as if they were the same measure. Report AI's benchmark used 19 binary classification datasets from the Grinsztajn et al. (2022) tabular benchmark (Hugging Face inria-soda/tabular-benchmark; mixed-feature versions where available), with classes balanced by the benchmark's authors. For each dataset and random seed we drew 1,000 training and 2,000 test rows (stratified), trained each model with library defaults (scikit-learn 1.9.1, XGBoost 3.2.0, LightGBM 4.7.0, CatBoost 1.2.10, TabPFN v2 weights via the tabpfn 10.0.0 package), standardised features for the four scale-sensitive models, and recorded accuracy, balanced accuracy, ROC AUC and time on a 4-core CPU without a GPU. The main ranking uses five splits per dataset for all 13 models (TabPFN costs 25–80 seconds per fit on a CPU); a 10-split run of the other 12 models is reported as a robustness check. Further experiments varied training size (100 to 10,000 rows, 3 seeds, TabPFN to 1,000 rows, on the 13 datasets with at least 6,000 rows per class), feature scaling (10 seeds), class balance (10% positives, 3 seeds, without TabPFN) and hyperparameter tuning (20-configuration random search with 3-fold cross-validation, 3 seeds, nine models). Categorical features were left in the benchmark's ordinal coding. All averages, ranks and shares are Report AI calculations (DERIVED); literature figures carry their source and confidence.

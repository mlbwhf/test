2. 1943–1993: rules and grants

The first thirty years ran on government grants and confident forecasts, and both collapsed when the promises came due

Alan Turing's 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence', published in October 1950, framed the question of machine intelligence as a test of behaviour and predicted that in about 50 years machines would play his imitation game well enough to fool an average interrogator much of the time. The term 'artificial intelligence' first appears in the proposal for the Dartmouth summer project, dated 31 August 1955, which asked for a '2 month, 10 man study'; the workshop itself ran in the summer of 1956.

Early results were striking for their time: Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron in 1958, the ELIZA conversation program in 1966, the Shakey robot at SRI. But the programs worked in toy worlds. In 1969 Minsky and Papert's 'Perceptrons' exposed the limits of single-layer networks, and the US Mansfield Amendment pushed military research funding towards direct applications. The UK's Lighthill report of 1973 judged that AI had failed to meet its promises, and in 1974 DARPA cancelled about $3 million a year of speech-understanding grants at Carnegie Mellon.

The second boom was commercial. Expert systems encoded specialist rules, and XCON configured computer orders for Digital Equipment Corporation; Japan's Fifth Generation project, launched in 1982 with a budget of about ¥54 billion over ten years, aimed at logic-programming machines. In 1987 the market for dedicated Lisp machines collapsed as cheaper general-purpose workstations caught up, and the second winter followed. One idea from this period outlived it: backpropagation, popularised in Nature in October 1986, became the training method of every modern neural network.

Why forecasts failed

The early forecasts were not foolish about ideas; they were wrong about inputs. The 1957 Perceptron used about 690,000 floating-point operations to train, by Epoch AI's estimate. The same quantity of compute is now spent in well under a millionth of a second on a single modern accelerator. Researchers could describe learning machines decades before they could afford to run them.

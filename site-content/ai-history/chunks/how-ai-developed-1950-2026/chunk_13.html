Taken together, the 2026 record does not support the video's conclusion that a model 'outmaneuvered the test'. It supports a narrower and still significant finding: a developer documented concerning behaviour by early versions in its own testing, withheld the model from general release, gave defenders a head start, and released a safeguarded version two months later. Whether that model of staged release holds as more labs reach the same capability is the open question for 2027.

Comparison

The current boom differs from the first two in who pays, what the core input costs and how many people use the result

The table compares the three booms on the quantities this report tracks. The first two were financed by governments and by a narrow set of corporate buyers, and each ended when its central technology hit a cost or capability ceiling: general-purpose reasoning programs could not scale beyond toy problems, and expert systems were brittle and tied to expensive specialised hardware.

The third boom has so far avoided both ceilings because its core input, compute, has kept getting cheaper per unit of capability while spending on it has risen. Its exposure lies elsewhere: in the concentration of investment, in electricity supply, and in whether revenue grows fast enough to justify capital spending. Our companion report applies Carlota Perez's framework for technological revolutions to that question.

Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“0.001% of a billion AI interactions is ten million incidents.”0.001% of 1 billion is 10,000. The '<0.001%' in Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview system card (April 2026) is an upper bound for concealment attempts by earlier versions in internal testing, not a rate for the released model. · detail
“The term 'artificial intelligence' was coined at the 1956 Dartmouth conference.”It first appears in the Dartmouth proposal dated 31 August 1955 (McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon). The workshop itself ran in summer 1956 — about 18 June to 17 August per Solomonoff's notes, six weeks in other accounts — and attendance varied; the proposal asked for a '2 month, 10 man study'.
“The AI winters were 1974–1980 and 1987–1993.”These are conventions. UK cuts followed the Lighthill report in 1973; DARPA's cuts came in 1973–74. For the second winter Wikipedia gives 1987–2000 while other histories use 1987–1993. Say 'roughly' and name the defining events (DARPA cuts; Lisp-machine collapse).
“Hinton invented backpropagation in 1986.”Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularised it for neural networks (Nature, 9 Oct 1986). The method appeared earlier: Linnainmaa's 1970 master's thesis (reverse-mode differentiation), Werbos's 1974 Harvard thesis, Parker (1985).
“Minsky and Papert's 'Perceptrons' (1969) killed neural-network research and caused the AI winter.”It was one factor among several. The 1969 Mansfield Amendment redirected DARPA money to mission-oriented work, and the Lighthill report (1973) hit UK funding. Sources do not establish a single cause.
“Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1996.”Kasparov won the February 1996 match 4–2 (Deep Blue won one game, on 10 Feb 1996). Deep Blue won the May 1997 rematch 3½–2½, with the decisive game on 11 May 1997.
“A chatbot passed the Turing test in 2014 (Eugene Goostman).”At a University of Reading event on 7 June 2014, 33% of 30 judges were fooled in five-minute chats by a bot posing as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy. That clears an organiser-chosen 30% bar, not a standard Turing set: his 1950 paper made a prediction (no more than a 70% chance of correct identification after five minutes by about 2000), not a pass criterion.
“Humans make 5.1% errors on ImageNet, so AI beat humans in 2015.”5.1% is one trained annotator (Andrej Karpathy) on a sample of validation images in 2014; two-thirds of his errors came from not knowing the 1,000 classes. ResNet's 3.57% (Dec 2015) beat that single estimate, not 'humans'.
“ChatGPT reached 100 million users in two months — the fastest-growing app ever.”The 100M figure was a UBS estimate of monthly users in January 2023 from Similarweb web-traffic data, not an OpenAI disclosure; OpenAI's own first 100M figure was weekly users in November 2023. Threads passed 100 million sign-ups in five days in July 2023 — sign-ups, not active users, so the two records are not comparable.
“GPT-4 has 1.76 trillion parameters.”OpenAI has never disclosed it. 1.76T traces to George Hotz's speculation; Semafor reported ~1T and SemiAnalysis ~1.8T. The mixture-of-experts design is also unconfirmed. Stanford's AI Index 2026 notes frontier labs have stopped reporting parameter counts. Note that even Epoch's database stores 1.8T in its Parameters field, explicitly flagged in its notes as a rumour — do not cite Epoch as confirmation.
“GPT-4 scored in the top 10% on the bar exam.”OpenAI's percentile compared GPT-4 with February Illinois bar takers, a group skewed towards repeat takers. A 2024 re-evaluation (Artificial Intelligence and Law) found a much lower percentile against first-time or all July takers — Live Science summarised it as not breaking the 70th percentile.
“AI training compute doubles every 3.4 months.”That was OpenAI's 2018 estimate for 2012–2018. Epoch's longer series gives ~5.7–6 months for the deep-learning era, about 4–5× a year for notable models since 2010 and ~5× a year for frontier language models since 2020. Before 2010 the doubling time was about 20 months.
“OpenAI's o3 solved 25% of FrontierMath.”25.2% was OpenAI's own December 2024 run, probably with more compute or scaffolding. Epoch's independent test of the released o3 in April 2025 found about 10%. OpenAI had funded the benchmark and had access to many problems, which Epoch disclosed only later.
“AlphaGo Zero used 3.41×10^23 FLOP and proves compute grows 10× a year.”3.41e23 is OpenAI's 2018 estimate and counts the compute spent generating self-play games. Epoch's database now counts only direct network training: 6.5×10^20 FLOP for AlphaGo Zero ('Confident') and 3.4×10^20 for AlphaGo Master. Quote the basis with the number; neither figure supports a '10× a year' trend claim.
“Google paid $500 million for DeepMind.”Terms were not disclosed. Reports ranged from $400 million (Re/code) to 'more than $500 million' (The Information).
“XCON saved DEC $40 million a year.”Most accounts put R1/XCON's savings at about $25 million a year; $40 million was DEC's figure for XCON plus related expert systems.
“Training frontier models is getting cheaper (AI Index 2026: −30%, ~$120M).”That figure comes from one secondary summary and could not be checked in the report (hai.stanford.edu unreachable). Epoch's trends page says frontier language-model training cost has risen ~3.5× a year since 2020, and its data put 2025 runs at $218M–$388M amortised (2023 USD) and Grok 4 at ~$490M nominal. Quote Epoch until the AI Index text is verified.
“The US produced 61 notable AI models in 2023.”61 is the AI Index 2024 count; the AI Index 2025 restated 2023 as 70. Quote the edition. (2024: 40; 2025: 50 or 59 depending on the 2026 PDF vs web page.)
“AI inference costs have fallen 280-fold.”Stanford's figure covers GPT-3.5-level capability from $20 (Nov 2022) to $0.07 (Oct 2024) per million tokens; it is not the price of frontier AI. · detail
“DeepSeek trained a frontier model for $5.6 million.”$5.58M was DeepSeek's stated GPU-rental cost for V3's final pre-training run only — excluding research, earlier runs, staff and hardware — and is not comparable with Epoch's ~$490M estimate for Grok 4.
“Claude Fable 5 was withdrawn within a week of launch (and is unavailable).”Anthropic suspended Fable 5 and Mythos 5 on 12 June 2026, three days after the 9 June launch, under a US export-control directive, and restored both on 1 July 2026 (Mythos 5 for a set of US organisations only). Fable 5.1 has been generally available since September 2026. · detail

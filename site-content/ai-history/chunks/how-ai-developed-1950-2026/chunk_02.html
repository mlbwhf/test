Analysis

The history of AI is usually told as a story of ideas: symbols versus neurons, rules versus learning. Measured instead in the quantities this site tracks, compute, cost, scores, money and users, it becomes a story of inputs. Every lasting advance since 2012 has come with a large increase in the compute and data spent on training, and every winter followed a period in which promises ran ahead of what the available hardware could deliver.

Three things distinguish the current boom from the two that ended in winters. First, it is paid for by revenue and private capital: global corporate AI investment reached $581.7 billion in 2025 according to the Stanford HAI AI Index 2026, while the first boom ran on defence grants worth a few million dollars a year. Second, the core input keeps getting cheaper: GPT-4-level output cost $37.50 per million tokens in March 2023 and about $0.20 in October 2026. Third, use is mass rather than specialist: more than a billion people use ChatGPT each week.

The comparison also shows where the risks sit. The expert-systems boom of the 1980s ended when specialised hardware was undercut by cheaper general-purpose computers and the systems proved brittle outside narrow tasks. Today's equivalent pressure points are visible in our own data: training costs measured in hundreds of millions of dollars per run, data-centre electricity that the IEA expects to more than double by 2030, and benchmark scores that saturate faster than they can be replaced, leaving less independent evidence of what new models can do.

The newest pattern is that the frontier is no longer the same thing as the market. In 2026 the most capable models were released first, or only, to vetted organisations, and one was briefly switched off by government order. For anyone using AI statistics, that means a model's existence, its benchmark scores and its availability now need to be dated separately.

Key takeaways
  • Use compute, not calendar years, to compare eras: the jump from AlexNet (2012) to GPT-4 (2023) alone was about 45 million-fold in training compute (Report AI calculation from Epoch AI estimates).
  • Winters followed promises, not failures of ideas: neural networks were set aside in 1969–1986 and returned once hardware and data caught up.
  • Prices fall fastest for a fixed level of capability; the frontier itself is priced high and rising for the best models.
  • Adoption figures need their definitions attached: weekly users, monthly users and 'organisations using AI in at least one function' are different measures.
  • From 2026, date a model's announcement, its general availability and any suspension separately.

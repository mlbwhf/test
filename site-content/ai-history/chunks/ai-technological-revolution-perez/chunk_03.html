2. Five surges

Five revolutions since 1771 followed the same sequence: big bang, installation, bubble, turning point, golden age

Perez dates the five surges from their big bangs: Arkwright's mill at Cromford in 1771; the trial of the 'Rocket' locomotive for the Liverpool–Manchester railway in 1829; Carnegie's Bessemer steel plant in Pittsburgh in 1875; the first Model T from Ford's Detroit plant in 1908; and Intel's microprocessor in Santa Clara in 1971. Each began in a core country, Britain for the first two, the United States and Germany for the third, and the United States for the fourth and fifth, before spreading.

Each produced a bubble tied to its infrastructure: canal mania (crash 1793), railway mania (1847), the global infrastructure bubbles that ended in the Baring and Argentine crises of 1890–93, the Roaring Twenties (1929), and, for ICT, what Perez calls a 'double bubble': the internet mania that burst in 2000 and the easy-credit boom that burst in 2007–08. The railway mania was the largest relative to its economy: at the 1847 peak UK railway investment reached about 7% of national income and nearly 55% of gross fixed capital formation, according to figures Perez cites.

From big bang to major crash took 22, 18, 15, 21 and 29 years for the five surges, an average of about 21 years (Report AI calculation from Perez's dates, using 2000 for ICT). For the fifth surge Perez says both the installation period and the turning point have broken their length records: the golden age she expected after 2008 has not yet begun (Exhibit 1).

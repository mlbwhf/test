3. Perez on AI

Perez's own verdict: AI is the latest stage of the ICT revolution, not a sixth one, though it could prepare the ground for one

Perez has addressed AI directly several times. In a March 2024 Project Syndicate essay, 'What Is AI's Place in History?', she wrote: 'Everyone is talking about artificial intelligence as though it represents the next technological revolution. In fact, it is better understood as a key development within the still-evolving information-communications-technology (ICT) revolution, which started in the 1970s with the microprocessor.' Her 2022 update of her surge table lists artificial intelligence, robotics and the Internet of Things among the fifth surge's industries.

Her argument turns on a distinction: 'a revolutionary technology is not the same thing as a technological revolution.' She compares recent AI advances to 'the introduction of plastics mid-way through the mass-production revolution' and describes AI as possibly a 'third leap' within ICT, after microprocessors and the internet. She also notes what is new: 'after four revolutions replacing manual labour, the new technologies have found an enormous new territory to mechanise: mental labour!'

She leaves room for change. AI, she wrote in 2024, 'could also lay the groundwork for breakthroughs in biotech, nanotech, and other fields that may be at the center of an eventual sixth revolution'. And she warned that if AI develops 'in a system where financial markets remain unregulated and decoupled from the real economy, it is unlikely to move us in a more environmentally, socially, or politically sustainable direction'. Asked about the AI boom in August 2025, she told the Financial Times: 'I have not seen a golden age happening without a crash.'

Not the 'fourth industrial revolution'

Perez's count differs from the popular 'Industry 4.0' or 'fourth industrial revolution' numbering promoted by the World Economic Forum. She counts five surges since 1771 and places today's digital technologies, AI included, in the fifth. The two numbering systems are not interchangeable.

4. The key factor

'Cheap intelligence' passes Perez's tests on cost and reach, fails on supply, and is unproven on productivity

If AI were a new surge, its key factor would be cheap intelligence: the price of a fixed level of model capability. On cost it passes easily. Epoch AI found in March 2025 that the price of reaching a given score fell between 9× and 900× a year depending on the benchmark, with a median of about 50×. On our own series, a dollar bought about 26,700 tokens of GPT-4-level output at GPT-4's launch in March 2023 and about 5 million in October 2026 (Exhibit 2). The underlying chips also keep getting cheaper: Epoch estimates AI chip performance per dollar has risen about 49% a year since 2023.

On pervasiveness it also passes: 89% of respondents to McKinsey's 2026 survey say their organisation uses AI in at least one function, 52% of US employees reported using it at work in May 2026, and ChatGPT passed 1 billion weekly users in August 2026.

Supply is where the test fails. Perez's key factors were, for practical purposes, unlimited: coal, steel, oil and chips could be produced in whatever quantity demand required. AI compute is not yet like that. Advanced packaging capacity at TSMC has had to be roughly doubled each year, North American data-centre vacancy fell to 1.4% in the first half of 2026, and the rental price of an Nvidia H100 rose from about $2.00 to $2.88 an hour between December 2025 and October 2026. Power is the binding constraint in many markets.

Productivity is unproven. Only 37% of McKinsey's 2026 respondents report any contribution to EBIT from AI. Daron Acemoglu's 2024 estimate put the total-factor-productivity gain over ten years at no more than 0.71%, and a 2025 randomised trial found experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on tasks with early-2025 AI tools. Other estimates are higher, and the evidence is moving, but it does not yet meet Perez's standard of 'a generally recognized capacity' to cut costs across the economy.

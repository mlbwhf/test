6. Where AI sits

Three readings fit the evidence; we use a hybrid, in which AI is a revolutionary technology inside the ICT surge that could seed a sixth

Applying the method leaves three defensible readings, set out in the table with the strongest argument for and against each. Reading A, AI as the sixth surge in its installation period, is the most common in markets and the press. Reading B, AI as a late chapter of the ICT surge, is Perez's own. Reading C combines them.

We use Reading C as our analytical frame, and it is our judgement, not Perez's classification. It follows her precedent of electricity, a revolutionary technology that spread inside the steel surge before becoming central to the next one. It explains why AI's key input is still the microchip and why ICT incumbents lead, while allowing that cognition priced by the token, agentic automation of mental work and the power infrastructure being built now could become the core of a sixth surge.

Timing follows from the reading. Under A, with ChatGPT as the big bang in November 2022, a crash in 2026 or 2027 would come only four or five years in, far earlier than the 15–29 years from big bang to crash in the five historical surges. It would resemble the 'precursor bubbles' Perez identifies in ICT's early years, such as 1987, more than an end of installation. Under B, the ICT turning point has lasted since 2000–2008, and Perez's view is that the golden age is held back by unregulated finance and lack of direction rather than by technology.

7. Deployment and institutions

A golden age, in Perez's account, depends on institutions catching up, and in 2026 they had only begun to

Deployment, in Perez's framework, is when the technology's benefits spread across the economy, and it requires a recomposition of institutions after the turning point: re-regulation of finance and policies that give the new paradigm a direction. For the fourth surge that meant Keynesian demand management, collective bargaining, social security and the Bretton Woods institutions. For the current surge she has argued for a 'smart, green, fair and global golden age', with the state providing 'directionality'.

The institutional record on AI in 2026 is partial. The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024, but its Omnibus regulation of July 2026 moved stand-alone high-risk obligations to December 2027, and no fines had been published by the end of September 2026. In the United States there is no federal AI statute; policy runs through executive orders, export controls and state laws, of which 145 were enacted in 2025 by one count. Export controls reached a frontier model for the first time in June 2026, when Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 were suspended for 19 days. Financial regulation of AI-related credit has not changed.

Deployment indicators are mixed. Use is broad, but measured gains are modest, and the labour effects are beginning to show: Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 121,401 announced US job cuts attributed to AI in January–October 2026, and employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations was about 19% lower relative to less-exposed groups by August 2026.

Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“AI is the fourth industrial revolution.”That numbering comes from the World Economic Forum's 'Industry 4.0' framing. In Carlota Perez's widely used framework there have been five technological revolutions since 1771, and she places AI within the fifth, the ICT revolution that began in 1971.
“The AI investment boom is bigger than the railway mania.”In dollars, yes; relative to the economy, no. UK railway investment reached about 7% of national income at the 1847 peak (figures cited by Perez, 2009); big-five tech capex was about 1.5% of US GDP in 2025 (Report AI calculation), and not all of it is for AI.
“Carlota Perez says AI is the sixth technological revolution.”She says the opposite: in March 2024 she wrote that AI 'is better understood as a key development within the still-evolving' ICT revolution, while allowing that it could lay the groundwork for a sixth.

Definitions

Terms used in this report

Technological revolution (surge)
In Perez's framework, a cluster of new technologies, industries and infrastructures, with a common key input, that transforms the whole economy over 40–60 years.
Big bang
A visible event that crystallises a revolution's opportunity, such as the first Model T in 1908 or Intel's microprocessor in 1971.
Key factor
The input whose relative cost collapses and which becomes cheap enough to reorganise production: coal, steel, oil, microelectronics.
Techno-economic paradigm
The 'common sense' of each surge about the most efficient way to organise production and firms.
Installation and deployment
The two halves of a surge: first the new technologies are installed, financed largely by speculative capital; then they spread across the economy, led by production capital.
Turning point
The crash and recession between installation and deployment, in which finance is re-regulated and institutions are recomposed.
Financial vs production capital
Perez's terms for capital seeking returns from financial assets versus capital invested in producing goods and services; they decouple in a frenzy and recouple in deployment.
Golden age
The synergy phase of deployment, when growth is broad-based, as in the Victorian boom or the post-war boom.

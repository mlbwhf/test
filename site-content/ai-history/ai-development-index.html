Analysis

This index tracks AI's development through six measures that can be dated and sourced across decades, rather than through a single composite score. Composite indices hide their weights; separate measures let readers see that capability, cost and adoption have moved at very different speeds. The companion report, How AI Developed, 1950–2026, tells the story; this page holds the numbers.

The clearest long-run signal is compute. Epoch AI's database of notable models shows the largest training run of each year rising by about 25 orders of magnitude since 1950, with a sharp change of pace around 2010: before then compute doubled roughly every 20 months, in line with general computing, and afterwards about every six months. Since 2020 the largest language-model runs have grown about 5× a year. The record in November 2026 is Epoch's estimate of about 9.4×10²⁶ FLOP for GPT-6 Astra, roughly 45 times GPT-4.

Cost and capability have moved in opposite directions for buyers and builders. For buyers, a fixed level of capability gets dramatically cheaper: GPT-4-level output fell about 187-fold in price between March 2023 and October 2026. For builders, the frontier gets dearer: Epoch's amortised estimate for the final training run of Grok 4 is about $390 million, against about $16 for AlexNet in 2012. Capital has followed the builders, with corporate AI investment rising about 29-fold in real terms from 2013 to 2025.

Adoption and risk measures are younger and less consistent. McKinsey's survey shows organisational use rising from 20% of respondents in 2017 to 89% in 2026, and ChatGPT passed 1 billion weekly users in August 2026. Incident counts are rising too, but they record what is reported, not what happens, and the main public database could not be fully refreshed for this edition.

Key takeaways
  • Compute is the longest consistent series: about 25 orders of magnitude from 1950 to 2026 in Epoch AI's largest-run-of-the-year data.
  • Growth sped up around 2010, from a 20-month doubling to roughly 6 months, and has run at about 5× a year for frontier language models since 2020.
  • A fixed capability gets cheaper fast; the newest frontier stays expensive to build and to buy.
  • Investment is cyclical even in this boom: corporate AI investment fell in 2022 and 2023 before more than doubling in 2025.
  • Compare like with like: weekly and monthly users, cloud-rental and amortised costs, and current and constant dollars are kept separate here.

1. Compute

Training compute: from 10⁶ FLOP for the Perceptron to about 10²⁷ for today's frontier

Training compute is the number of arithmetic operations used to train a model. It is the measure most closely linked to capability gains in the deep-learning era and the one with the longest consistent record. All values are Epoch AI estimates; labs rarely disclose them, and Epoch rates each estimate's confidence.

Growth rates: Epoch estimates a doubling roughly every 20 months before 2010 and about every 6 months afterwards (about 4–5× a year), and about 5× a year for frontier language models since 2020 (90% interval 4–6×).

Fact check

Numbers to stop quoting

Widely repeatedWhat the evidence supports
“The term 'artificial intelligence' was coined at the 1956 Dartmouth conference.”It first appears in the Dartmouth proposal dated 31 August 1955 (McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon). The workshop itself ran in summer 1956 — about 18 June to 17 August per Solomonoff's notes, six weeks in other accounts — and attendance varied; the proposal asked for a '2 month, 10 man study'.
“The AI winters were 1974–1980 and 1987–1993.”These are conventions. UK cuts followed the Lighthill report in 1973; DARPA's cuts came in 1973–74. For the second winter Wikipedia gives 1987–2000 while other histories use 1987–1993. Say 'roughly' and name the defining events (DARPA cuts; Lisp-machine collapse).
“Hinton invented backpropagation in 1986.”Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularised it for neural networks (Nature, 9 Oct 1986). The method appeared earlier: Linnainmaa's 1970 master's thesis (reverse-mode differentiation), Werbos's 1974 Harvard thesis, Parker (1985).
“Minsky and Papert's 'Perceptrons' (1969) killed neural-network research and caused the AI winter.”It was one factor among several. The 1969 Mansfield Amendment redirected DARPA money to mission-oriented work, and the Lighthill report (1973) hit UK funding. Sources do not establish a single cause.
“Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1996.”Kasparov won the February 1996 match 4–2 (Deep Blue won one game, on 10 Feb 1996). Deep Blue won the May 1997 rematch 3½–2½, with the decisive game on 11 May 1997.
“A chatbot passed the Turing test in 2014 (Eugene Goostman).”At a University of Reading event on 7 June 2014, 33% of 30 judges were fooled in five-minute chats by a bot posing as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy. That clears an organiser-chosen 30% bar, not a standard Turing set: his 1950 paper made a prediction (no more than a 70% chance of correct identification after five minutes by about 2000), not a pass criterion.
“Humans make 5.1% errors on ImageNet, so AI beat humans in 2015.”5.1% is one trained annotator (Andrej Karpathy) on a sample of validation images in 2014; two-thirds of his errors came from not knowing the 1,000 classes. ResNet's 3.57% (Dec 2015) beat that single estimate, not 'humans'.
“ChatGPT reached 100 million users in two months — the fastest-growing app ever.”The 100M figure was a UBS estimate of monthly users in January 2023 from Similarweb web-traffic data, not an OpenAI disclosure; OpenAI's own first 100M figure was weekly users in November 2023. Threads passed 100 million sign-ups in five days in July 2023 — sign-ups, not active users, so the two records are not comparable.
“GPT-4 has 1.76 trillion parameters.”OpenAI has never disclosed it. 1.76T traces to George Hotz's speculation; Semafor reported ~1T and SemiAnalysis ~1.8T. The mixture-of-experts design is also unconfirmed. Stanford's AI Index 2026 notes frontier labs have stopped reporting parameter counts. Note that even Epoch's database stores 1.8T in its Parameters field, explicitly flagged in its notes as a rumour — do not cite Epoch as confirmation.
“GPT-4 scored in the top 10% on the bar exam.”OpenAI's percentile compared GPT-4 with February Illinois bar takers, a group skewed towards repeat takers. A 2024 re-evaluation (Artificial Intelligence and Law) found a much lower percentile against first-time or all July takers — Live Science summarised it as not breaking the 70th percentile.
“AI training compute doubles every 3.4 months.”That was OpenAI's 2018 estimate for 2012–2018. Epoch's longer series gives ~5.7–6 months for the deep-learning era, about 4–5× a year for notable models since 2010 and ~5× a year for frontier language models since 2020. Before 2010 the doubling time was about 20 months.
“OpenAI's o3 solved 25% of FrontierMath.”25.2% was OpenAI's own December 2024 run, probably with more compute or scaffolding. Epoch's independent test of the released o3 in April 2025 found about 10%. OpenAI had funded the benchmark and had access to many problems, which Epoch disclosed only later.

Epoch's database now counts only direct network training: 6.5×10^20 FLOP for AlphaGo Zero ('Confident') and 3.4×10^20 for AlphaGo Master. Definitions

Terms used in this index

Training compute (FLOP)
Total floating-point operations used to train a model; Epoch AI estimates it from hardware, training time and utilisation, or from parameters and training data.
Notable model
A model in Epoch AI's database that meets its notability criteria (state of the art, highly cited, historically significant or widely used).
Amortised training cost
Hardware depreciation plus energy for the final training run, in constant dollars (Epoch AI method).
Cloud-rental training cost
The price of renting the same compute from a cloud provider; typically two to four times the amortised cost.
Constant 2021 dollars
Values adjusted for inflation to 2021 prices, so years can be compared directly.
Weekly active users
People who used a service at least once in a seven-day period; not comparable with monthly active users.

Frequently asked

What is the AI Development Index?

Report AI's AI Development Index is a dated, sourced record of how AI has developed since 1950, tracked through six measures: training compute, capability (benchmarks), cost, capital, adoption, and risk and resources. It is updated quarterly and the full dataset is free to download as a CSV.

Why not combine the measures into one score?

A single score would need weights that are arbitrary and would hide the fact that the measures move at very different speeds. Keeping them separate lets readers see, for example, that prices for a fixed capability fall while frontier training costs rise.

What is the largest AI training run so far?

Epoch AI's estimate for OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, released in September 2026, is about 9.4×10²⁶ floating-point operations, roughly 45 times GPT-4. Epoch rates the estimate 'Likely', with a range of about 5×10²⁶ to 2×10²⁷.

How fast is AI compute growing?

Epoch AI estimates that training compute for frontier language models has grown about 5× a year since 2020 (90% interval 4–6×), and that compute for notable models doubled about every six months after 2010, against about every 20 months before.

How much is invested in AI each year?

The Stanford HAI AI Index 2026 puts global corporate AI investment at $581.7 billion in 2025 in current dollars, of which private investment was $344.7 billion. In constant 2021 dollars, Our World in Data's series shows corporate investment of about $490 billion in 2025, up from about $17 billion in 2013.

Methodology & sources

Figures are compiled from company disclosures, regulators, research firms and reputable reporting, and cross-checked against Report AI's own index pages. Each value carries its as-of date, source and a confidence rating: HIGH where the primary source was read, MEDIUM where it rests on consistent reputable secondary reporting (most figures, because many primary pages cannot be opened from our research environment), DERIVED where it is our own arithmetic, and CONTESTED where credible sources disagree. A year means the calendar year the figure describes; 2026 values are the latest available and marked “to date”. Weekly and monthly user counts, and forecasts and outcomes, are never compared as if they were the same measure. The AI Development Index draws on Epoch AI's Notable AI Models dataset (downloaded 12 November 2026) for compute, parameters and training costs; Our World in Data's republication of the Stanford HAI AI Index for investment and model counts; the AI Index for prices, benchmarks, incidents, publications and patents; McKinsey's Global Survey on AI for organisational adoption; OpenAI for ChatGPT users; and the IEA, LBNL and Gartner for electricity. The decade scoreboard, growth multiples and the 187-fold price fall are Report AI calculations (DERIVED). Values that could not be checked against a primary source in this edition are rated MEDIUM; the AI Index 2026 summary claim of about $120 million for the largest mid-2025 training run is CONTESTED because Epoch's estimates for 2025 runs are higher. These reports compile and verify secondary data with original analysis; they are not original surveys. See our editorial standards, corrections log and fact-check register.

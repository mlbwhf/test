Analysis

Why the market-size numbers disagree — and which one to use

Ask three analysts the size of the AI video market and you will get three honest, incompatible answers. The narrowest definition — revenue of pure text-to-video generation engines like Kling, Runway and Veo API sales — puts 2026 at roughly $847–946 million. Add AI-assisted editing and post-production software and the 2026 figure grows to about $3.67 billion. Draw the widest boundary — the full AI video platform stack including avatar tools, dubbing/localization, and enterprise video suites — and the market was already ~$6.2 billion in 2025, the base for Grand View Research's projection of $47.8 billion by 2034 at a 25.4% CAGR.

All three are "right"; they measure different things. The practical rule: use the narrow figure to size the model-provider opportunity, the middle figure for the creative-software market, and the broad figure for the total category. What no definition disputes is the trajectory — usage grew ~840% in two years, adoption reached 78% of marketing teams, and per-clip generation costs fell to cents, the classic precondition for a volume market.

The forecast's biggest tension is unit economics. Video inference is the most compute-hungry generative workload, and the market's most famous product — OpenAI's Sora — is being retired in 2026 partly because a standalone app could not monetize against those costs. Growth to $47.8B assumes inference costs keep falling faster than prices — plausible given the trend, but it is an assumption, not a fact.

Key takeaways
  • Three markets, three numbers. ~$0.9B (generation engines), ~$3.7B (+ editing), ~$6.2B (full platform stack) — always check the boundary before quoting a figure.
  • The growth is real regardless of definition. ~25% CAGR to 2034 is anchored by verified usage growth, not just analyst optimism.
  • Compute cost is the forecast's fault line. Sora's shutdown shows what happens when inference costs outrun monetization.

Demand drivers — and the three risks to the forecast

What powers the growth: 78% of marketing teams already use AI video at least quarterly and 73% of the Fortune 500 have integrated it into workflows; per-clip costs of $0.05–$0.84 per 10 seconds make video cheap enough to test like display ads; and localization — regenerating one spot across dozens of markets — turns every global campaign into a volume customer. Full usage data lives in our AI Video Generation Statistics index.

What could break it: (1) Compute economics — video inference costs are the highest of any generative workload, and Sora's exit shows what happens when monetization lags them. (2) Copyright litigation — training-data suits against video models could impose licensing costs the forecasts do not include; see our report on AI and copyright. (3) Trust and regulation — deepfake and provenance rules (EU AI Act transparency obligations among them) could constrain the consumer segment; our deepfake fraud index tracks the misuse side of the same technology.

FAQ

How big is the AI video market in 2026?

Between ~$0.9B and ~$7.8B depending on definition: $847–946M for pure generation engines, ~$3.67B including AI editing, and ~$7.8B (derived) on the broad platform definition growing from $6.2B in 2025.

How fast is it growing?

25.4% CAGR on the broad definition (Grand View Research), reaching a projected $47.8B by 2034. Underlying usage grew faster still — generation volume rose ~840% between January 2024 and January 2026.

Who leads the market?

By documented revenue, Kling (~$500M run-rate); by distribution and volume, Google's Veo/Flow ecosystem; by professional tooling, Runway. OpenAI's Sora exits the market by September 2026.

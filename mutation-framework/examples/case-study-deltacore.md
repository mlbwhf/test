# Case Study: DeltaCore

**Type:** Cautionary · **Status:** v1.0-draft (composite/illustrative — flag for owner review)
**Profile:** ~2,800-person industrial equipment manufacturer with a growing software division

> Case studies in this directory illustrate the framework in use. Names and
> details are composites drawn from field experience and the companion
> books; they are teaching cases, not audited accounts.

## Starting position

DeltaCore scored **44/72 — Mutation-Aware** on its first scorecard, run as
a personal scan by the Chief Digital Officer. Per the framework's warning,
the personal scan skewed optimistic: a team scan three months later came in
at 36, with Leadership Posture at 3/12 — the lowest score the facilitators
had recorded.

The signal that should have organized everything was already in hand:
two of DeltaCore's largest customers had begun pilots with a competitor
whose service contracts were priced dynamically using telemetry and AI.
This appeared in DeltaCore's own account reviews — twice — without being
logged anywhere or escalated to anyone.

## What they did — and where it went wrong

**They bought tools instead of pulling levers.** The response to the AI
Fluency score was an enterprise AI platform license and a training catalog.
Eighteen months later, AF1 ("AI embedded in everyday workflows") had moved
from 2 to 3; the platform's weekly active usage was under 4% of seats. No
workflow had a measured before/after delta — the deployment had owners for
procurement, none for outcomes.

**They installed the cadence, then let the calendar eat it.** The weekly
signal review ran for nine weeks. It died the way such meetings die: a
quarter-end crunch ("just this week"), a senior skeptic's standing
conflict, a junior owner who couldn't compel attendance. By month four the
SignalNet had become a write-only document.

**They ran the Mutate column from the core P&L.** The dynamic-pricing
response was eventually funded — as a line item inside the services
division whose margins it threatened. The division ran it with the
diligence a body applies to a transplant it intends to reject: staffed with
people who could be spared, reviewed by the executives it would disrupt.
It produced a pilot, a deck, and no decision, for five consecutive
quarters. Kill criteria had never been written, so there was nothing to
trigger; the initiative could neither succeed nor die.

**The honest KPI vanished first.** Kill latency was on the original
dashboard. After two quarters of embarrassing values, it was dropped in a
deck revision nobody remembers deciding on — exactly the failure mode the
KPI scorecard warns about.

## Result

By the second annual reassessment, DeltaCore scored 33 — *lower* than its
honest baseline. Both pilot customers signed multi-year contracts with the
competitor. The software division's best engineers, hired for the
transformation that kept not arriving, left at twice the company average.

## The lesson

DeltaCore failed in the framework's terms before it failed in the market's:
**a signal logged nowhere, a cadence with no protected owner, a mutation
bet funded by the organ it threatened, and a leadership team that deleted
the metric instead of moving it.** Every individual decision was locally
reasonable. That is the cautionary point — Mutation-Blindness at the end
was not a lack of information. The information showed up twice. It was a
lack of any mechanism obligated to act on it.

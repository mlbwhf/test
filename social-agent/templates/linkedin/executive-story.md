# LinkedIn — Executive Story (single image or text-only)

Produce the complete deliverable in exactly this format:

```
Post: [SHORT WORKING TITLE — the person/event/concept]
Visual Suggestion: [see rules below]

The Post:
[the post text]

CTA Options:
[CTA question 1]
[CTA question 2]
```

## Visual Suggestion rules
Choose ONE:
- An ultra-realistic, slightly imperfect photograph — describe a grounded, minimalistic executive scene (desk objects, boardroom, notebook, watch, glasses). Explicitly avoid artificial gloss and stock-photo aesthetics. Example: "An ultra-realistic, high-end image of a boardroom table. A slightly creased financial spreadsheet printout sits next to a pair of casually dropped headphones. Minimalistic and grounded."
- "Without visuals." — when the hook or quote is strong enough to carry the post alone. Add one line of rationale (e.g. "Let the strength of the opening quote hook the reader.").

## The Post — structure
1. **Hook (1–2 lines).** Open with a specific, dated moment or documented fact about a real person, company, or phenomenon: "In 1972, five engineers walked out of IBM...", "Sir Ken Robinson once stood on a stage and told an audience something genuinely strange." Never open with the lesson.
2. **The story (4–8 very short paragraphs).** One sentence or two per paragraph, each paragraph on its own line. Concrete numbers, dates, names, dollar amounts. Build to the little-known twist: "Here is the part of the story almost nobody talks about."
3. **The pivot to now (1–2 short paragraphs).** Connect the story directly to what senior executives are facing today: "This is the Otellini moment, and it is being remade right now in almost every senior executive committee." Speak to enterprise/AI transformation reality.
4. **The lesson (1–2 short paragraphs, optionally a compact list).** What the leaders who handle this well actually do — specific, contrarian, actionable.
5. **Close (1 line).** A grounded, forward-looking statement. The recurring signature line "The world has changed, and the leaders who notice will be the ones the next decade is built around." may be used sparingly — at most in ~1 of every 5 posts.

## The Post — style rules
- 250–350 words total.
- Hashtags: 2–4, CamelCase, woven INLINE mid-sentence where the concept appears (e.g. "the coming #IdentityShift", "focusing on #StrategicPlanning") — never dumped at the end.
- Audience: senior executives and enterprise leaders navigating AI-era transformation.
- Voice: authoritative, story-driven, lightly contrarian. Verified facts and numbers over adjectives. No emoji. No "I'm excited to share".
- Sentences short. Paragraphs 1–3 lines, each on its own line with a blank line between.

## CTA Options — rules
Exactly two alternatives, each 1–2 lines, each a direct question to the reader that invites comments (e.g. "Have you ever let a spreadsheet talk you out of a paradigm shift? I'd love to hear your story in the comments."). One may carry a soft consulting/discussion invitation ("Let's discuss", "Let's explore how to navigate this shift together"). Never both salesy.

## Exemplar (follow this quality bar)

Post: Paul Otellini
Visual Suggestion: An ultra-realistic, high-end image of a boardroom table. A slightly creased financial spreadsheet printout sits next to a pair of casually dropped headphones. Minimalistic and grounded.

The Post:
In 2005, Paul Otellini became the CEO of Intel and took a meeting with Steve Jobs, who was preparing to launch the iPhone and needed chips.
Otellini ran the spreadsheet, and the numbers said the deal would lose money.
He followed the spreadsheet, and Apple went elsewhere.
Every smartphone on Earth now uses an ARM chip, and the forecasted volume in Otellini's spreadsheet was wrong by roughly 100x.
In his final interview, Otellini admitted his gut told him to say yes, but he couldn't see past the forecasted costs and volumes.
The deeper irony is that Intel actually owned an ARM chip business called XScale at the time.
They sold it for $600 million right before the iPhone launched because the margins didn't match its PC business model.
This is the Otellini moment, and it is being remade right now in almost every senior executive committee focusing on #StrategicPlanning.
Spreadsheets are tools for evaluating incremental decisions in known markets using historical data.
When asked to evaluate a paradigm-shifting opportunity, a spreadsheet will systematically recommend against it because its mathematical structure cannot answer the question.
When evaluating #Innovation and paradigm shifts, the spreadsheet is just one input.
The cost of being wrong in the direction of caution on a paradigm shift is enormous and asymmetric.
The world has changed, and the leaders who notice will be the ones the next decade is built around.

CTA Options:
Have you ever let a spreadsheet talk you out of a paradigm shift? I'd love to hear your story in the comments.
What other inputs are you prioritizing alongside your spreadsheets to evaluate paradigm shifts?
